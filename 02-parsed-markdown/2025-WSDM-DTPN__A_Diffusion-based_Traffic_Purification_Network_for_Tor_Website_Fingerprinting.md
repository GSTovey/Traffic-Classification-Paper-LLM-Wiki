## Abstract

Website Fingerprinting attack is a type of method used to classify network trafic generated by users on the Tor (The Onion Router) based on the websites they visit, leading to the leakage of individ uals’ privacy . For Website Fingerprinting attack, network trafic defense methods involve adding noise to the original network traf fic to render the attacker’s methods inefective. Previous attack methods primarily focused on improving classification accuracy by enhancing the attack model, with adversarial training being the most common approach. However, adversarial training requires fre quent updates and exhibits poor generalization when dealing with previously unseen network trafic protection methods. In order to address the limitations of adversarial training, a novel method is proposed leveraging a difusion model for network trafic purifica tion. This paper is the first to use a difusion model to resist network trafic defense based on adversarial perturbations. The difusion models are theoretically suited for data purification in the training mode, i.e., removing noises generated by adversarial perturbations from the data. Our method enables existing network trafic classifi cation methods to maintain efective classification of network trafic after protection without requiring retraining, while also achieving good generalization performance with previously unseen network trafic defense methods. The purified network trafic data can effectively improve the robustness of existing website fingerprinting methods. Experiments conducted under various network trafic defense strategies demonstrate that the proposed method increases accuracy by up to 60.8% on DF dataset and 50.3% on CW100 dataset, respectively, compared to adversarial training.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Research on network trafic includes techniques such as trafic prediction[1]and trafic classification, aimed at enhancing data management and privacy protection. Trafic classification, in particular, is a form of privacy mining, as it involves extracting insights from network data while potentially exposing sensitive information. Additionally, web trafic and log analysis play a crucial role in understanding user behaviors and improving website performance, while ensuring that user privacy is maintained.

Tor (The Onion Router) [2] is an anonymous communication network designed to enable users to browse and transmit data on the Internet while preserving their anonymity, including that of their web trafic. However, despite its ability to ofer substantial privacy, the trafic generated by Tor users remains vulnerable to a type of attack called Website Fingerprinting (WF).

Prior WF methods [3–7] often rely on hand-crafted features for performing WF attacks. However, current methods [8–10] pre dominantly employ deep learning-based methods. These methods eliminate the cumbersome process of feature engineering, acceler ate the deployment of WF attacks.

Network Trafic Protection (NTP) methods combat WF attacks by introducing noise into the original data flow.This strategy includes adding spurious packets, altering packet content, or introducing delays to undermine the attacker’s methods. Existing measures for NTP fall into two principal categories: trafic obfuscation [11–15] and adversarial samples [16–20]. Trafic obfuscation adds noise at the application or network layer but often results in high compu tational overhead or inadequate protection against specific trafic classifications. The most efective current defense involves adver sarial perturbations, where minimal noise is introduced to Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) during training, leading WF classifiers to misclassify trafic.

Current methods for network trafic classification use neural networks, which are vulnerable to adversarial attacks. These attacks exploit minor input changes to deceive the network into making incorrect predictions, reducing the efectiveness of neural networks in WF attacks. To bypass NTP defenses, WF attackers use Adver sarial Training, which targets specific inputs and is impractical for full network flows. This strategy struggles with frequent updates and new protection methods, and its limitations against protections like AdvTrafic [19] have been demonstrated.

To address the challenge, we introduce a novel network trafic purification method centered on generative models in this study. Our proposed method empowers established network trafic classification methodologies to proficiently categorize safeguarded net work trafic without necessitating retraining eforts. Furthermore, it demonstrates robust generalization capabilities for novel NTP methodologies that have not been previously encountered. The key contributions of our work are as follows:

• We pioneer the application of difusion models in WF and introduce the concept of trafic purification to boost WF attack capabilities. Our Difusion-based Trafic Purification Network (DTPN) <sup>1</sup> model is the first one proposed and applied in the field of trafic purification.

• Drawing inspiration from the UNet architecture, we combine TransformerBlock and one-dimensional convolution to de sign a sophisticated UNetFormer, which is utilized as a noise estimator in our introduced difusion model. Furthermore, in consideration of the unique requirements of our task, we introduce a novel loss function to enhance the generalization of model.

• We use DTPN to obtain purified trafic and apply it to test WF attack methods, achieving significantly better results. In experiments conducted on two datasets, we achieve a maximum improvement of 60.8% and 50.3% compared to attacks without trafic purification.

## 2 RELATED WORK

## 2.1 WF Attacks

Starting from the initial proposal by Herrmann et al. to use the Naive Bayes method for WF attacks on Tor [3], the use of machine learning-based approaches becomes more prevalent. For instance, in [4], [21], and [5], SVM and KNN are respectively employed for classification. With the advancement of deep learning techniques, Sirinam et al. [8] developed Deep Fingerprints (DF) for Tor browsers based on complex Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Subsequently, in [22] and [10], the classifiers are also enhanced based on DF. Additionally, Bhat et al. [9] introduced Var-CNN for WF using the ResNet-18 architecture.

## 2.2 WF Defenses

Trafic Obfuscation. These defense mechanisms are designed to make network trafic more dificult to classify. Early defense measures [11, 12] used constant-rate padding to reduce information leakage caused by timing intervals and trafic patterns. However, these methods resulted in significant bandwidth overhead, exceeding 150%. Recently, research eforts have aimed to reduce this overhead by inserting packets at positions with large time intervals between packets (WTF-PAD [13]). However, WTF-PAD achieved protection success rates of only 9%, when facing strong DNN-based network trafic classification attacks. Supersequence Defense [14, 15] attempted to find a supersequence, which is a long sequence of network flows containing subsequences from diferent website network flow sequences. The most efective supersequence defense, Walkie-Talkie, achieved a 50% success rate in protecting against DNN attacks.

Adversarial Perturbations. Goodfellow et al. first introduced evasion attacks against DNNs, where adversaries introduce small adversarial perturbations [23] to the input of a DNN, causing it to misclassify the input. Recent research on NTP [16, 17] has used adversarial perturbations to counter DNN-based attacks. However, adversarial perturbation-based NTP faces the challenge of requiring prior knowledge of the entire future network flow to compute the necessary adversarial perturbation before establishing a connection. Mockingbird [17] addressed this limitation by calculating adversarial perturbations using a collected network trafic database and applying them to new network trafic. Meanwhile, Nasr et al. [18] proposed precomputing universal adversarial perturbations for unknown network trafic to achieve real-time protection. However, attackers can enhance the robustness of their models through adversarial training [24, 25], leading to a significant drop in the protection rate to 66.7% [19]. Furthermore, Minipatch [20] reduced bandwidth overhead by employing adversarial patches. AdvTrafic [19] utilized AdvGAN [26] for adversarial training against WF attackers, and experimental results indicated that adversarial training is no longer efective for AdvTrafic.

## 2.3 Difusion Models

The generative model is a type of model that can stochastically generate observed values based on latent parameters. In recent years, notable difusion-based generative models have been proposed, including difusion probabilistic models [27], Noise Conditional

Score Networks (NCSN) [28], and Denoising Difusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) [29]. These models have been applied across various fields, including recommendation[30], knowledge graphs[31], and so on. DifPure [32] proposes a difusion model to undermine adversarial samples in computer vision. With the difusion forward and reverse processes, such small noises can be removed. The state-of-the-arts of WF defenses are based on adversarial samples (perturbations). Inspired by DifPure, we leverage the difusion model to remove the perturbations in Tor burst sequence.

## 3.1 Threat Model

In a black-box attack scenario against an NTP method, the adversary can only collect network flow data before and after the protection method is applied, without access to specifics of the implementation. Moreover, if NTP employs a neural network as its foundation, the model architecture and parameters themselves can not be obtained by attackers. The black-box assumption precludes the extraction of gradient information or other insights that could compromise the defense or facilitate more potent attacks. Under this constrained view, the protections are a black box to wouldbe adversaries, limiting their ability to precisely reverse engineer vulnerabilities or craft evasions finely tuned to specific weaknesses.

## 3.4 Training DTPN

To train DTPN, we adopt the training method proposed by Song et al. in VP-SDE [35]. During the training phase, a random time step � ∈ [0, 1] is sampled, and the variance scheduler $\beta _ { t }$ of the noise is adjusted to obtain the corresponding noise variance at the current time step. Subsequently, the data $\mathbf { x } _ { t }$ with noise at the �-th time step during the forward difusion process, was combined with the time step � and the conditional embedding $e ,$ and fed into the score estimator network $\pmb { s } _ { \theta }$ to estimate the noise. The underlying principle is that difusion models based on both Bayesian theory and score models $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , SDE) have a common essence. The Bayesianbased difusion model utilizes neural networks to estimate the noise $\epsilon _ { t }$ at the �-th time step, whereas the SDE-based difusion model uses neural networks to predict $- \frac { \epsilon _ { t } } { \sqrt { 1 - \bar { \alpha } _ { t } } }$ , difering only by a coeficient. Thus, the mean squared error between the estimator network output and the noise can serve as the first loss function, which can be formulated as follows:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {1} = \left\| S _ {\theta} (\pmb {x} _ {t}, t, \pmb {e}) + \frac {\epsilon_ {t}}{1 - e ^ {2} \sigma_ {t}} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}\tag{7}
$$

Given that our objective is attacks against NTP methods that rely on adversarial perturbations in the WF classification task, we require a well-trained WF model to guide the training process of the difusion model. The guidance component can be expressed using the second loss function, which can be formulated as follows:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {2} = C E \left(y, \phi^ {\mathrm{WF}} \left(\boldsymbol {x} _ {t} - \sigma_ {t} ^ {2} \boldsymbol {s} _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {x} _ {t}, t, \boldsymbol {e})\right)\right)\tag{8}
$$

,where CE(·) denotes the cross-entropy function.

The total loss is:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {t o t a l} = \mathcal {L} _ {1} + (1 - t) \tau \mathcal {L} _ {2}\tag{9}
$$

,where � is a scalar hyperparameter.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS

## 4.1 Dataset and Experimental Settings

In this paper, we used two publicly available large-scale datasets, i.e., the DF dataset [8] and the CW100 dataset [38]. The DF dataset comprises 95 categories of websites, with each website having 1,000 network flows. The dataset was collected by visiting the homepages of all the top 100 Alexa sites for 1,250 times. The CW100 dataset consists of 100 diferent categories of websites, with each website containing approximately 2,500 network flows. In both the DF and CW100 datasets, each network flow (i.e., each data sample) is a sequence composed $\mathrm { o f } + 1$ and 1, representing network data packets sent between the client and server. Subsequently, we perform data preprocessing, all the 95 websites in the experiment are used with 460 network flows from each website. Similarly, after preprocessing the CW100 dataset, websites with only 54 network flows in a category are removed, and 2,425 network flows from each of the remaining websites are selected for the experiment. Based on statistical analysis and model performance, we choose the network flows (samples) length of 512.

Additionally, we refer to the existing work on web trafic protection and use DF as the default WF model $\phi ^ { \mathrm { W F } }$ . The DF model used in this paper is provided in [8]. This approach is designed for blackbox attack scenarios, and it necessitates the collection of network flow data both before and after the utilization of the NTP (Network Time Protocol) technology. Specifically, this involves gathering the pure data � and the adversarial samples $x _ { a }$ to construct a dataset $\{ ( x , x _ { a } , y ) \}$ . We implement the model using PyTorch, and all the experiments are conducted on a server with 128 GB of memory and four NVIDIA 3090 GPUs. In the specific implementation, the number of discretization steps $N = 1$ , 000 for the difusion process, the step length $\Delta t = - \frac { 1 } { N }$ for the antagonistic data purification process, the number of training iterations $N _ { \mathrm { e p o c h } } = 5 0$ , and batch size = 512 are set.

## 4.2 Experiments on Adversarial Training under Diferent NTP Methods

In this experiment, we conduct experiments on four mainstream and state-of-the-art network trafic defense methods. Walkie-Talkie [15] and WTF-PAD [13] represent the two mainstream trafic obfuscationbased NTP methods. Mockingbird [17] and AdvTrafic-AdvGAN [19] are published in 2021 and 2022, respectively. They use a deep learning-based approach as an adversarial perturbation-based method for NTP.

We first evaluate the attack performance on defended data, where the DF model is trained on non-defended data and tested on defended data. The resulting accuracy is referred to as WF test accuracy. Next, we conduct adversarial training, where the DF model is both trained and tested on defended data. This experimental setup reflects a more realistic scenario in which a website fingerprinting attacker identifies the applied defense and retrains its classifier using defended data. The resulting accuracy is referred to as adver sarial training accuracy. Finally, we apply our method (DTPN) by using defended data as input and purifying this data. The output is purified data. We then use the purified data as the dataset to test the trained DF model, and the resulting accuracy is referred to as our method accuracy. The dataset is split into 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% testing.

As shown in Tables 2 and 3 , Walkie-Talkie [15] has the lowest classification accuracy after trafic purification on the DF and CW100 datasets, with only 60.1% and 57.7% respectively, which is because Walkie-Talkie adds 149.6% bandwidth overhead to the network trafic, resulting in the loss of a large number of global discriminative features on the defensed network trafic. For the latest study, AdvTrafic-AdvGAN [19], the method is capable of efectively reducing the impact of adversarial training. As a result, even after retraining the network trafic data protected by AdvTrafic-AdvGAN using adversarial training, the classification accuracy of WF attackers on the DF and CW100 datasets can only reach 10.2% and 24.9%, respectively. This is basically unsuccessful for the WF classification task to identify user behaviors from the network trafic generated during the use of the onion network. Us ing the difusion model-based network trafic purification method, the WF classification accuracy can be improved to 71.0% and 75.2% on the DF and CW100 datasets, respectively, making previously used attack methods usable again. ## 4.4 Ablation Study

The difusion model proposed in this paper for network trafic purification mainly has the following innovations compared to the original difusion model: (a) the hidden layer features of the WF classification model are input to the difusion model as the conditional embedding of the difusion model; (b) the loss function of the WF classification model is introduced as an auxiliary task for the training of this method. Also for the UNetFormer used in the difusion model as a fractional network, the hyperparameters of the UNetFormer network applied in this method are adopted similarly to the convolutional hyperparameters of the DF model, so that the global features of the network trafic can also be learned better. This subsection includes three experiments to showcase the efectiveness of the method proposed, which are (a) removing the conditional embedding, (b) removing the WF model auxiliary task, and (c) replacing the UNet with a three-layer MLP.

## (3) Variance scheduler $\beta _ { 0 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 }$

The variance scheduler parameters $\beta _ { 0 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 }$ are used as hyperparameters of the variance scheduler to control the amount of noise added to the data during the forward difusion process. In addition, a linear variance scheduling function is used. For the noise variance at time step �, it is calculated as $\beta _ { t } = \beta _ { 0 } + t \left( \beta _ { 1 } - \beta _ { 0 } \right)$ , where $\beta _ { 0 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 }$ denote the noise variance at the beginning and end of the forward difusion moments, respectively, and $\beta _ { 0 } < \beta _ { 1 }$ . If the variance scheduling factor is taken too small, it will cause both the training and inference process of the model to use more difusion time steps for the malicious semantic information of the counteracting noise to be completely eliminated. If the variance scheduling factor is too large, it may cause the model to add too much noise to the data during the forward difusion process, making too much local semantic information of the data lost, and thus the fractional network model cannot converge well.

## (4) Guidance weight � for the WF model

When applying the inverse difusion process to recover adversar ial data with added difusion noise, the difusion model needs to use an existing WF recognition model to guide its output. Otherwise, the difusion model cannot learn good feature information and thus cannot recover data that are not adversarial and can be correctly classified by the WF recognition model. In the experiments of this subsection, the WF recognition model used is the DF model, and its loss function used in the training process is the cross-entropy loss function. How to balance the loss function of the difusion model itself and this auxiliary loss function needs to be adjusted using a weight �. In this paper, the values of the WF recognition model guidance weight � are taken from 0.0 to 40 in uniform intervals, and a total of 10 values are taken. From Figure 6 , it can be observed that as � increases from 0 to 20, the test accuracy of the difusion model steadily improves overall, although there is some minor fluctuation. However, beyond � values of 32, there is a significant decrease in accuracy. Therefore, this paper adopts 20 as the final value for the WF model guidance weight �.

Based on Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 in [33], it becomes evident that local adversarial noise within adversarial data can be eradicated during the forward difusion process and subsequently restored during the reverse difusion process, leading to the generation of non-adversarial data. Additionally, clean data should not introduce excessive noise, as an excessively high level of noise can also eliminate the underlying global label semantics of the data. Therefore, it is necessary to find an optimal purification difusion time step $t ^ { * }$ The optimal difusion time step $t ^ { * }$ is determined by the trade-of between purifying local perturbations (with larger $t ^ { * } )$ and preserving global structures of adversarial data (with smaller $t ^ { * } ) ,$ to achieve high robust classification accuracy. From Figure 7 , it can be seen that when the optimal purification difusion time step $t ^ { * } = 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 } ,$ the WF classification model $\phi ^ { \mathrm { W F } }$ achieves the highest accuracy of 71.0%. As $t ^ { * }$ increases, the accuracy of $\phi ^ { \mathrm { W F } }$ decreases. Moreover, as $t ^ { * }$ increases, the time consumed for data purification increases linearly, so we can use smaller $t ^ { * }$ as much as possible to reduce the resource overhead of the purification process while ensuring the accuracy of $\phi ^ { \mathrm { W F } }$ . Therefore, $t ^ { * } = 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ is the optimal choice.

## 5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

As trafic protection methods based on GANs have emerged, adversarial training has proven to be inefective in enhancing the adversarial robustness of WF models. To improve the performance of existing WF attack methods, we introduce difusion models to purify adversarial data and counter NTP methods. Experimental results demonstrate substantial improvements achieved by the proposed approach against both mainstream and state-of-the-art NTP methods, surpassing the performance of adversarial training. Difusion-based methods use reverse difusion to recover clean data from noisy data. The noise addition involves smaller step size and numerous iterations of model inference, which can make it slower than methods such as GANs. It is potential to speed up network trafic purification.
