# QuicCourier: Leveraging the Dynamics of QUIC-Based Website Browsing Behaviors Through Proxy for Covert Communication

Jianan Huang , Weiwei Liu , Member, IEEE, Guangjie Liu , Member, IEEE, Bo Gao , Member, IEEE, and Fengyuan Nie

Abstract—Network covert channels transmit secret messages by manipulating network traffic, including packet headers, timing intervals, and communication patterns. The growth of network services has spurred interest in exploring these channels. Yet, the practical application of these channels faces challenges in transmission rate and reliability due to unpredictable network interference. QUIC-based websites offer promising opportunities for covert communication, given their inherent dynamic nature from web resource updates and network interferences. Repeated visits or refresh actions on the same website generate substantial statistical redundancy. Furthermore, widely used proxy tools introduce additional traffic morphology changes. This paper presents QuicCourier, a covert channel leveraging web traffic’s dynamic characteristics and proxy service encapsulation to hide messages in QUIC packets from the service node to the client. Guided by a generative model for web resource patterns, QuicCourier ensures that covert traffic closely resembles legitimate traffic, employing three packet-wise meta operations. The altered QUIC flows are then encased in proxy protocols, complicating the detection ofembedded information. The covert receiver is incorporated into the proxy client. The efficacy of QuicCourier is evaluated using a dataset of over 30,000 web browsing traffic samples, demonstrating its exceptional undetectability against state-of-the-art traffic classification tools and a high covert transmission rate.

Index Terms—Network covert channel, packet manipulation, proxy service, QUIC, Traffic analysis.

In general, any communication data, resource states, or communication behaviors accessible to participants of a covert channel can serve as carriers. With the accelerating pace of the Internet, the prevalence of network packets has spurred the evolution of network covert channels. The effectiveness of a network covert channel is determined by three key performance metrics: undetectability, reliability, and covert transmission rate. To maintain resilience against a wide range of network traffic analysis tools and address inherent network interference, the covert transmission rate and reliability have emerged as primary constraints that impact the practical applicability of network covert channels.

Network covert channels are typically classified into two main categories: covert storage channels (CSCs), which use network protocol fields or payloads for covert communication, and covert timing channels (CTCs), which manipulate network packet timing information for covert purposes. However, CSCs are vulnerable to detection through traffic normalization and behavior analysis [2]. Moreover, the growing use of encryption protocols has constrained the evolution of CSCs, as more protocol fields are now encrypted. Furthermore, CTCs exhibit a heightened vulnerability to network latency and packet loss, which renders them challenging to operate effectively within real network environments. Due to the rigid structure of lower-layer protocols like IP, TCP, and UDP, as well as the variability of Internet jitter, an increasing number of upper-layer protocols are being studied for constructing network covert channels [3], [4], [5], [6].

In general, if any discernible element of network communication behavior can be manipulated to encode secret data while maintaining consistency with similar observable samples, a network covert channel can be established. In recent years, the construction of covert channels leveraging various network services has gained significant attention [4]. Common carriers include network multimedia and online games. For instance, a multimedia covert channel scheme proposed in [5] embeds secret messages by replacing encoded video frame data in video calling applications. However, this approach is vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks, as the transmission passes through uncontrolled application gateways. Similarly, for online games, decentralized game environments have been utilized to create covert channels between a user and the game server by replacing non-essential in-game messages [6]. Nonetheless, these methods are highly application-specific and heavily dependent on the state and behavior of the selected application.

It is well established that ideal carriers for covert channels in upper-layer network applications should be both ubiquitous and exhibit dynamic statistical characteristics. Among these, web browsing stands out as one of the most prevalent forms of network communication. The frequent updates to website content naturally introduce dynamic spatio-temporal traffic patterns, which not only complicate passive traffic analysis techniques, such as website fingerprinting, due to concept drift [7], [8], but also create significant opportunities for covert channels. Specifically, this inherent variability facilitates packet-level manipulations by leveraging the coarse-grained distribution of packet length-related features, enabling the embedding of secret data within web browsing traffic.

A key challenge in implementing such packet manipulation techniques lies in the complexity of modifying the underlying network stack to intercept traffic, embed secret data, and reencapsulate it [9]. However, the increasing reliance on proxy services for web access offers a practical and efficient alternative [10]. Proxies are widely used for mitigating IP blacklisting, bypassing geo-restrictions, and automating web crawling, as well as for providing encryption to thwart deep packet inspection (DPI) [11]. Beyond these conventional applications, proxies can also be leveraged by individuals, including participants in covert communication, to actively engage in covert channels. Acting as intermediaries, proxies provide a natural interface for implementing packet manipulations during the encapsulation process, eliminating the need for modifications to the network stack.

Despite these advantages, proxy services still leak metadata, such as payload lengths and traffic directions, which can be exploited by wardens using statistical methods to infer user behaviors [12]. Therefore, for covert channels to achieve broad applicability, they must demonstrate resilience to scrutiny from unencrypted proxies and robustness against DPI. These requirements demand both behavioral consistency to preserve spatiotemporal characteristics and packet-level compliance with protocol specifications.

With the widespread adoption of HTTP/3 and its underlying QUIC protocol, QUIC traffic, with its inherent dynamic characteristics, has emerged as a suitable carrier for covert channels, offering several advantages for packet manipulation in the modulation of secret data. First, the packet header in QUIC traffic exposes only the minimal fields necessary for parsing, simplifying packet manipulation. This design enables modifications without requiring additional adjustments to exposed protocol fields, thereby reducing the complexity of covert packet manipulation. Second, QUIC’s architecture, which operates over UDP, ensures that each packet is self-contained. This allows individual packets to be manipulated without causing anomalies or inconsistencies that might compromise the integrity ofthe session. Furthermore, QUIC’s acknowledgment mechanism, which incorporates redundant packets, provides an efficient feedback channel. This mechanism can be leveraged to establish reliable covert communication by offering a robust method to confirm the receipt and integrity of secret data. Additionally, the QUIC protocol incorporates extensive state management within its encrypted messages, further enriching the dynamic statistical patterns inherent in legitimate traffic. As a result, the integration of the QUIC protocol with proxy services holds significant potential for enabling high-capacity, secure, and reliable covert channels.

In this paper, we present QuicCourier, an innovative proxybased covert channel that leverages the dynamic spatio-temporal characteristics of QUIC-web browsing traffic, combined with the encapsulation capabilities provided by commonly used web proxy services. It facilitates the covert transmission of secret messages from the proxy node to the client through the manipulation of QUIC data within the proxy packets. This is achieved by employing a packet manipulation method guided by the characterization generative model for web resource pattern. The implemented meta-operations at the packet level encompass packet padding, packet insertion, and packet replacement, thereby preserving the inherent characteristics of web browsing traffic. In addition, we utilize the ACK packets in QUIC, which naturally include redundant frames, to address potential packet loss issues. These ACK packets provide feedback from the client to the proxy node, enhancing the reliability of the covert transmission.

The main contributions of this study can be outlined as follows:

\- A novel framework for constructing network covert channels by combining packet manipulation and network proxy services is presented in this study. This framework leverages the prevalent behavior of accessing websites through proxies and capitalizes on the dynamic spatio-temporal characteristics of browsing traffic for the same website. Additionally, the encapsulation capabilities of web proxy services naturally facilitate inherent packet-wise operations. This framework provides a means to integrate various web services with web proxies, facilitating high-capacity covert communications in actual networks.

\- A method for QUIC packet manipulation, guided by a characterization generative model for web resource pattern, has been developed within the proposed covert channel framework. This method encompasses two algorithms: Web Signal Separation and Recombination (WebSpare) and Payload Length Selection (PLeS). The WebSpare algorithm generates website resource patterns represented by run-length sequences of maximum payload size (MPS) packets. Concurrently, the PLeS algorithm ascertains the optimal length for packet insertion, crucial for maintaining the spatio-temporal characteristics of the traffic. This innovative method facilitates highly secure and reliable transmission of secret messages within web-browsing behavior.

In evaluating the proposed covert channel scheme, both general and capability-maximizing adversarial models have been employed, utilizing a real-world dataset that includes over 30,000 samples of website browsing traffic. The general model emulates standard detection capabilities, whereas the capability-maximizing model exemplifies the most advanced detection potential. Notably, under the capability-maximizing adversarial model, even when employing the advanced detection tool NeuTic, the detection accuracy for the proposed covert channel scheme registers at only about 60%, while achieving an average covert transmission rate of 4 bytes per packet.

## II. RELATED WORK

In this section, we provide an overview of network covert channels, focusing on service-independent and servicedependent types based on their reliance on specific network services. Additionally, we discuss various traffic identification methods potentially detecting these network covert channels.

## A. Network Covert Channels

Over the past few decades, significant advancements have been made in the field of network covert channels, exploiting both network packets [13] and commonly used upper-layer services to conceal secret data by leveraging the inherent redundancy in network traffic [14]. Table I provides a comprehensive summary of existing covert channel schemes, highlighting four key aspects: dependency on network service, carrier object, type of modulation technique, and overview of modulation method.

Service-independent covert channels primarily leverage redundancies in network protocols or packet timing characteristics to modulate secret information. A common strategy for covert channels is CSCs, which exploit unused or reserved fields in protocols like TCP and IP to embed secret messages [15]. IPv6 extended headers [16] and multiple fields in the TLS handshake [17] are also used to hide data. However, as TLS formal data transmission involves fewer plaintext headers, and more headers are encrypted in emerging protocols like QUIC [34], the effectiveness of these methods is diminishing. Wardens can also sanitize or modify plaintext headers, further reducing their utility. To address these issues, DYST [18] uses ARP or RTCP packets as signals when the hash of a legitimate packet matches a portion of the secret message. While this approach enhances security, the scheme must wait for appropriate legitimate packets to appear.

To overcome reliance on specific protocol types, CTCs manipulate packet timing to encode secret data. One method encodes messages through inter-packet delays, though this is sensitive to network jitter [19]. Cloak [20] mitigates this by dispatching packets across multiple flows in distinct patterns. Similarly, LiHB [21], is an HTTP-based covert channel that discreetly transmits secret data by dispatching the number of requests in flows, though its covert bandwidth is low. Another example, Li et al. [22] developed a covert channel for RTP-based video calls by encoding secret messages within the sequence numbers of intentionally dropped packets. Despite their resilience, these timing-based methods can create detectable traffic patterns, making them vulnerable to statistical and machine learningbased detection [35], [36].

In another, Balboa [23] utilizes the extracted session key to decrypt TLS packets, replacing the payloads with a pointer and secret data. The receiver then extracts the data and uses the pointer to restore the original data before sending it to the application. However, the dynamic nature of web traffic necessitates frequent updates to the pointer [37]. Similarly, Oakley et al. [24] propose a format transformation encryption (FTE) to tunnel covert traffic through host protocols that are difficult to block. Nevertheless, it limits in the static protocol with fixed spatio-temporal pattern for practical application.

The diversity of network services has led to the emergence of service-dependent covert channels, where services like multimedia streaming, cloud storage, and online games are exploited for covert communication. Multimedia applications, characterized by their substantial redundancy space, have played a pivotal role in the network covert channels. Instances like DeltaShaper [25], Facet [26], and CovertCast [27] encode secret data into live stream, often overlook anomalies introduced in the encoding process, thereby potentially elevating the risk of detection [38]. To minimize these risks, Protozoa [5] uses a hook technique to replace portions of encoded multimedia data with secret messages during WebRTC encapsulation. Stegozoa [28] builds on this approach by embedding secret data into multimedia frames encoding to avoid WebRTC gateway censorship. Although hook-based methods provide high security and substantial capacity, their effectiveness depends heavily on the status of applications and devices. Moreover, their implementation often requires extensive permissions, limiting their practical applicability.

Online games have also been leveraged to establish network covert channels. Systems like Castle [29] and Rook [30] embed secret messages into in-game behaviors, but rely on player actions for encoding, which results in limited bandwidth. In contrast, Telepath [6] achieves higher bandwidth by replacing non-disruptive messages with secret data during synchronization actions. To summarize, game-based covert channels generally lack universality, being confined to their specific design for a particular application.

Additionally, covert channels have also been developed using other Internet application services. SWEET [31] serves as an email tunnel that encapsulates traffic within email messages sent via Gmail. Another covert channel, Stealth [32], utilizes WebDav and Git for secret data transmission. In [4], a covert channel is implemented within the cloud service DropBox, enabling the exchange of covert information through file and metadata modification. Additionally, Camoufler [33] uses platforms like WhatsApp and Telegram to route web traffic through text messages, benefiting from the platforms’ low latency. Although these methods blend well with legitimate traffic, they are vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks and often degrade service quality due to the low real-time nature of the channels.

In summary, the rise of encrypted protocols and packet headers has reduced the effectiveness of CSCs. While CTCs offer more flexibility by manipulating flow temporal behavior, they struggle to preserve legitimate spatio-temporal traffic patterns. With the rapid evolution of network services, techniques such as QUICstep [39] leverage encrypted tunnels to obfuscate sensitive handshake information and employ QUIC’s connection migration to maintain seamless communication in the presence of censorship. This illustrates how core features of modern network protocols can be strategically utilized to evade restrictions while preserving performance. Similarly, covert channel construction has increasingly explored leveraging specific network services to enhance stealth and reliability. However, ensuring that these covert channels remain indistinguishable from legitimate traffic remains a critical challenge, necessitating further innovation in utilizing common network services for secure and resilient covert communication.

## B. Network Traffic Identification

Network traffic identification technique depends on fixed fields and deep-level traffic characteristics to ascertain its source or type, playing a vital role in detecting network covert channels. For web browsing traffic via proxy, the identification methods can be categorized into website fingerprinting techniques, which utilize inherent IP, domain, and other spatio-temporal characteristics presented in the traffic of different websites to identify their respective origin sites. However, with the widespread adoption of network proxies, content delivery networks, and encryption protocols, IP or domain-based traffic identification methods are increasingly becoming outdated [40]. Modern techniques for web browsing traffic identification primarily focus on extracting statistical features and applying classical machine learning strategies, alongside incorporating traffic representation in deep learning models. In [41], a multinomial Naive Bayes classifier is used to capitalize on the frequency distribution of packet sizes, offering robustness against minor website alterations. In [42], support vector machines (SVM) are utilized, extending the feature representation to include characteristics like total transmitted bytes and the ratio of downstream to upstream packets, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy of traffic classification. In the realm of classical machine learning-based traffic identification algorithms, AppScanner (AppS) is a notable method [7]. It analyzes packet length sequences and utilizes a set of 54 statistical features derived from upstream, downstream, and complete traffic, achieving an identification accuracy of96% across 110 applications.

In contrast to feature engineering methods combined with classical machine learning, network traffic identification techniques based on deep learning eliminate the need for manual feature design. These methods, utilizing deep learning, have shown superior performance in identifying traffic across various public datasets. For example, a study in [43] introduces FS-Net, an encrypted traffic classifier utilizing a double-layer bidirectional gate recurrent unit (GRU). FS-Net excels in extracting deep features from packet length sequences and functions as an end-to-end classifier within a comprehensive framework, showing outstanding performance on a real-world dataset encompassing 18 applications. Deep Fingerprinting (DF) [44], a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model for website fingerprinting attacks against Tor, employs direction sequences as input and achieves a 98% accuracy rate in closed-world scenarios. Additionally, EBSNN [45], a long short-term memory (LSTM)-based model, is designed to handle unknown complex applications. It utilizes side-channel and payload features, employing hierarchical attention networks to learn high-level representations. In [46], an advanced deep learning approach named NeuTic is presented, specializing in the identification of TLS traffic with packet sequences as its primary input. This model integrates a multi-kernel convolution technique for processing variable-length features and a sequence self-attention mechanism, effectively capturing long-range dependencies in packet sequences.

## III. SYSTEM DESIGN

In this section, we define the adversary model for our proxybased covert channel framework, covering both typical and maximal capability scenarios. Then, we introduce the framework designed to establish a network covert channel, enabling the transmission of secret messages through proxy traffic by manipulating packets.

## A. Adversary Model

Fig. 1 illustrates the adversarial scenario. Here, a user accesses a specific website through a proxy client, which collaborates with a proxy node to enhance Internet privacy. The proxy node serves as an intermediary, forwarding requests and retrieving website resource packets from the target website. This represents a standard privacy-enhancing web browsing activity via a proxy. However, within this seemingly normal activity, there exists a covert channel between the user and the proxy node. The proxy node, operated by a covert sender, discreetly transmits secret data to the user within the web browsing traffic via proxy.

The proposed covert channel scheme aims to mimic ordinary web browsing via proxy, ensuring undetectability, reliability, and practicality. For undetectability, the covert channel must avoid introducing abnormal network behavior or communication patterns, and the carrier communication should ideally be ubiquitous. Its traffic should closely resemble normal traffic across multiple dimensions of features, remaining indistinguishable even under advanced detection methods. For reliability, mitigating interference and packet loss is crucial, requiring a covert feedback mechanism to support acknowledgment and retransmission signaling, ensuring reliable communication under various network conditions. Lastly, for practicality, the design should minimize system modifications, utilize widely available network services, and ensure compatibility with existing devices, reducing deployment costs while maintaining normal service quality and availability.

For the purposes of our analysis, we assume that a warden can capture and analyze all network flows, including side-channel information such as packet payload, packet size, packet arrival time, and packet direction. In this scenario, covert transmissions are impermissible, whereas privacy-enhancing communication via proxy is viewed as a normal behavior. The warden possesses the capability to deduce the websites accessed by the proxy client via traffic identification tools.

In real-world scenarios, network traffic censorship typically begins with the detection of anomalies, followed by more detailed analyses. Reflecting this reality, we have developed two distinct adversarial models: the general and capabilitymaximizing models. These models are intended to simulate varying levels of capabilities and goals a warden may have in different operational environments.

In the general adversarial model, the warden’s goal is to ascertain if the traffic originates from a proxy of a commonly accessed website. This aligns with the primary goals of most current web traffic monitoring systems. To achieve this goal, the necessary capabilities include having labeled samples of proxy traffic from a large number of common websites (e.g., websites listed in the Alexa Top Rankings), and the ability to train these labeled samples using traffic identification tools to construct a model that can associate proxy traffic with its target website. The warden remains oblivious to the specifics of the covert channel. The covert sender needs to ensure that the covert traffic is recognized by traffic identification tools as corresponding to the common target website of its carrier traffic, rather than being seen as an unknown proxy traffic sample, thereby preventing further analysis by the warden.

In contrast to typical capability scenarios within the general adversarial model, the capability-maximizing adversarial model empowers the warden with maximal capabilities. Specifically, the warden possesses a thorough comprehension of the covert channel method and precise labels for both legitimate and covert traffic samples. The warden’s objective is to differentiate between legitimate and covert proxy traffic destined for a particular website. To accomplish this, a binary traffic identification tool is trained on these two sample categories to develop a decision model capable ofdetecting the existence ofa covert channel. The covert channel is effective as long as the covert traffic remains indiscernible from legitimate traffic, even when the warden has access to labeled samples and advanced traffic identification tools. Therefore, covert traffic should exhibit similar characteristics to legitimate proxy traffic.

In addition, it is important to note that the warden employs passive techniques for network traffic analysis, refraining from active actions like packet delaying or dropping. Additionally, the decryption of the packets is not practically feasible. In general, the warden is assumed to access all network traffic but lacks the ability to access third-party devices within the network or decrypt traffic from third-party sources. This represents the typical maximum capability that a warden can attain in practice.

## B. Overview ofQuicCourier

The overview of QuicCourier is illustrated in Fig. 2. This framework serves as a network covert channel that can seamlessly integrate into a proxy system, such as Socks5, functioning as a pluggable transport layer. QuicCourier allows users to access websites through proxies while clandestinely receiving secret data from the proxy node. The dynamic spatio-temporal characteristics of web browsing traffic not only expand the feature space for traffic classifiers, making the detection of specific targets more complex but also create opportunities for establishing a network covert channel. Leveraging the inherent asymmetry of web browsing traffic, QuicCourier utilizes downstream traffic from the proxy node to the client to covertly embed secret data into the proxy traffic. This method of secret data embedding is achieved through packet manipulation, guided by a generative model characterizing web resource patterns. The packet manipulation method encompasses the WebSpare and PLeS algorithms. The WebSpare algorithm generates website resource patterns represented by run-length sequences of MPS packets. Concurrently, the PLeS algorithm determines the optimal length for packet insertion, which is crucial for preserving the spatio-temporal characteristics of the traffic.

In this scenario, the client functions in dual capacities: as both the proxy client and the covert receiver. Similarly, the proxy node acts as the proxy server and the covert sender. During website browsing by the client, the proxy node initiates requests for the desired web content and then relays the web resources to the client. For QUIC traffic proxying, a virtual network interface is essential. This interface sets up a logical gateway compatible with UDP packets, enabling all QUIC packets to be directed to this virtual gateway and subsequently rerouted to the local proxy port.

When packets return from the proxy node to the client, QuicCourier applies packet manipulations to embed secret data. To preserve the integrity of web content, QuicCourier’s proxy node utilizes packet padding $F _ { P }$ and packet insertion $F _ { I }$ for downstream packets, while packet replacement $F _ { R }$ is employed to incorporate feedback bytes into the upstream packets, thereby enhancing the reliability of the covert channel. To ensure broad applicability, these manipulations should resist protocol analysis even under unencrypted proxies. As the secret data is typically encrypted, either through pre-encryption or by the proxy’s encryption layer, it remains indistinguishable from the encrypted payload. The primary focus is to ensure that the manipulated packets remain compliant with protocol specifications. Below, we define three fundamental packet manipulation techniques in QUIC that effectively preserve protocol conformity while embedding secret data.

Packet Padding $F _ { P } ( i , n ) $ : Padding the secret data of length n into the end of the i-th packet $p _ { i }$ . QUIC’s encryption ensures that most of the packet, including the header, is protected, exposing only minimal fields like flags and connection IDs. As a result, packet padding does not require any header modifications, preserving the packet’s integrity. In contrast, performing the same manipulation in popular TLS implementations requires adjusting the exposed length field in its header, which increases complexity and the risks of detection.

Packet Insertion $F _ { I } ( i , n )$ : Inserting a covert packet of n-byte secret data payload with the packet header into the front of the i-th packet $p _ { i } .$ . QUIC allows this inserted packet without disrupting the connection state due to its connectionless design and independent flow control management. In comparison, TLS, which operates over TCP, relies on maintaining a global connection state. TLS messages often span multiple packets, and inserting a packet requires to adjust the exposed headers of previous packets to maintain the correct connection state. This process is complex and error-prone, particularly if the packets have already been transmitted.

Packet Replacement $F _ { R } ( i , n )$ : Replacing the payload of the i-th packet with secret data of the length n. QUIC incorporates ACK packets for packet loss recovery, and its acknowledgment mechanism includes cumulative acknowledgment information, which mitigates the impact of potential ACK packet loss. By exploiting this cumulative nature, ACK packets can sporadically be replaced with a small amount of feedback bytes to enhance the reliability of the covert channel. The proxy node subsequently extracts the feedback and intentionally drops the packet, simulating a scenario where the packet is lost due to network interference.

By utilizing the run-length sequence of MPS packets as the characterization of web resource patterns, we employ the WebSpare algorithm for traffic feature space construction, traffic feature modeling, and traffic feature sampling. The generated run-length sequence of MPS packets serves as the pattern that guides the packet padding and packet insertion operations. With this MPS-related pattern, each downstream packet corresponds to an expected payload length $\boldsymbol { L } _ { i }$

Considering $n _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ to represent the maximum payload size of the UDP traffic, when the i-th packet’s payload $l _ { i }$ is smaller than $n _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ , and the expected payload length $L _ { i } = n _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ , packet padding $F _ { P }$ is employed to fill the packet with secret data up to the maximum payload size. Conversely, if the expected payload length $L _ { i } < n _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ , and the current packet payload length $l _ { i } = n _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ , packet insertion $F _ { I }$ is used to insert a covert packet before $p _ { i }$ with specific payload length determined by the PLeS algorithm. In cases where the expected payload length $L _ { i }$ equals the current packet payload length $l _ { i } ,$ it indicates the consistent behavior of a resource or resource block transmission, negating the need for further packet manipulation. The method consists of two key algorithms: the Web Signal Separation and Recombination (WebSpare) algorithm and the Payload Length Selection (PLeS) algorithm. The WebSpare algorithm is responsible for generating a run-length sequence of maximum payload size (MPS) packets, effectively capturing the web resource transmission characteristics within the downstream traffic. ## A. WebSpare Algorithm

Within the downstream traffic of QUIC web browsing, a noteworthy characteristic is the intermittent appearance of non-MPS packets after several consecutive MPS packets. This phenomenon stems from variations in web resources and forms a vital distinguishing element in different web browsing traffic patterns. Consequently, it becomes imperative to characterize and maintain this type of feature as a prerequisite for the covert channel. In this study, we utilize a run-length sequence of MPS packets to depict this characteristic, as it can accurately represent the web resource pattern. It’s worth noting that this resourcerelated feature typically exhibits stability over short time periods while displaying dynamic behavior over more extended time periods.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, to obtain the run-length sequence of MPS packets, the payload length sequence of downstream packets is initially partitioned based on the non-MPS packets, with each segment representing a resource or resource block in website browsing traffic. Given the downstream traffic containing $T$ non-MPS packets, the run-length sequence of MPS packets can be represented by:

$$
\mathbf {x} = [ x _ {1}, x _ {2}, \ldots , x _ {T} ]\tag{1}
$$

where $x _ { i }$ denotes the number of MPS packets between the (i − 1)-th non-MPS packet and the i-th non-MPS packet. The run-length sequence x of MPS packets, hereinafter also referred to as the MPS-related pattern for convenience.

In the WebSpare algorithm depicted in Fig. 4, run-length sequences from historical traffic samples of a specific QUIC website are used to build a generative model of web resource patterns. Fig. 4(a)-(c) show these sequences of MPS packets (or MPS-related patterns), denoted as $x ,$ extracted from payload length sequences of downstream packets from the same website, denoted as S. Each sequence is a discrete time series where each value corresponds to a run-length of MPS packets. These sequences, although originating from the same site, are influenced by dynamic factors such as network interference. They comprise both inherent intrinsic component $\mathbf { \sigma } _ { \mathbf { 0 } } = [ b _ { 1 } , \dots , b _ { T } ]$ and external dynamic component $\mathbf { e } = [ e _ { 1 } , \dots , e _ { T } ] .$ expressed as follows:

$$
\mathbf {x} = a _ {1} \mathbf {b} + a _ {2} \mathbf {e}\tag{2}
$$

where $a _ { 1 }$ and $a _ { 2 }$ denote the corresponding weights in the MPSrelated pattern x, respectively. After determining b and $\mathbf { e } ,$ the subsequent step involves generating the MPS-related pattern $\mathbf { y } _ { \mathrm { g e n } } ,$ which can be achieved through a summation of the two components. However, variability in $a _ { 1 }$ and $a _ { 2 }$ across sequences poses a challenge to the consistency of the generated patterns, and fixed weights can result in poor diversity. To address this issue, the proposed WebSpare algorithm incorporates a grouping strategy that maximizes reproducibility among groups, as depicted in Fig. 4(d)-(e).

To effectively implement the WebSpare algorithm, it is crucial to initially define the global parameters $( N _ { I } , N _ { P } )$ , which influence grouping and partitioning. The parameter $N _ { I }$ indicates the number of sequences in each group. Typically, the run-length sequences within these groups share similar collection times or originate from the same devices. On the other hand, the parameter $N _ { P }$ represents the size of each segment within run-length sequences. The segment is defined by a consecutive series of MPS packets and a non-MPS packet.

Upon completing the grouping and partitioning, MPS-related patterns extracted from the traffic samples of the same website are represented as a tensor $\pmb { \mathcal { D } } = ( \mathcal { D } ) _ { g , i , s , p } ^ { - } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { N _ { G } \times N _ { I } \times N _ { S } \times N _ { P } }$ In this tensor, $g \in \{ 1 , \ldots , N _ { G } \}$ represents the group index, and $N _ { G }$ indicates the total number of groups. Correspondingly, i ∈ $\{ 1 , \dots , N _ { I } \}$ signifies the index within a group, $s \in \{ 1 , \ldots , N _ { S } \}$ refers to the segment index within a sequence, while $N _ { S }$ denotes the total number of segments per sequence. Additionally, $p { \in } \{ 1 , \ldots , N _ { P } \}$ specifies the position within each segment. Furthermore, $\mathcal { D } _ { g , i , s , }$ is defined as the s-th segment of the i-th run-length sequence in the $g \cdot$ -th group for convenience. In subscript notation, a colon in a dimension signifies selecting all elements in that dimension.

For each group $\mathcal { D } _ { g }$ , the generated MPS-related pattern $\mathbf { y } _ { \mathrm { g e n } } ,$ which depicts the web resource transmission characteristics of the target website, can be defined as:

$$
\mathbf {y} _ {\mathrm{gen}} = \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {N _ {S}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {I}} w _ {i, s} \mathcal {D} _ {g, i, s,:} = \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {N _ {S}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {I}} (w _ {i, s} a _ {1, i, s} \mathbf {b} _ {s} + w _ {i, s} a _ {2, i, s} \mathbf {e} _ {s})\tag{3}
$$

In the s-th segment, $w _ { i , s }$ denotes the weight corresponding to the i-th sequence within this segment. The parameters $a _ { 1 , i , s }$ and $^ { a _ { 2 , i , s } }$ represent the intrinsic and dynamic component weights in the s-th segment of the i-th sequence, respectively. The intrinsic component in the s-th segment is represented by $\mathbf { b } _ { s } .$ whereas $\mathbf { e } _ { s }$ is the dynamic component in the same segment. When the conditions $\begin{array} { r } { \dot { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N _ { I } } } w _ { i , s } a _ { 1 , i , s } = 1 \mathrm { a n d } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N _ { I } } w _ { i , s } a _ { 2 , i , s } = 0 } \end{array}$  are considered in each segment, it enables the extraction of the intrinsic component. Similarly, the dynamic component is extracted using the same approach. Generally, direct extraction of these components presents challenges. Instead, inter-covariance is employed to approximate them and generate MPS-related patterns for each segment.

To efficiently extract distinct components and characterize the correlation relationships between $\mathcal { D } _ { g _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathcal { D } _ { g _ { 2 } }$ , the inter-group covariance $C _ { g _ { 1 } , g _ { 2 } }$ is utilized. This method involves summing covariances across all possible group combinations, revealing behavioral dependencies and similarities in resource transmission among various groups. The inter-group covariance can be calculated as follows:

$$
\sum_ {g _ {1} \neq g _ {2}} C _ {g _ {1}, g _ {2}} = \sum_ {g _ {1} \neq g _ {2}} \sum_ {i _ {1}, i _ {2}} w _ {i _ {1}} w _ {i _ {2}} \operatorname{Cov} \left(\mathbf {v} _ {g _ {1}, i _ {1}}, \mathbf {v} _ {g _ {2}, i _ {2}}\right) = \mathbf {w} ^ {T} \mathbf {U} \mathbf {w}\tag{4}
$$

In this formulation, the indices $g _ { 1 } , \ g _ { 2 }$ range over the set $\{ 1 , . . . , N _ { G } \}$ , while $i _ { 1 } , i _ { 2 }$ span $\{ 1 , . . . , N _ { I } \}$ . Here, $\mathbf { v } _ { g _ { 1 } , i _ { 1 } }$ denotes the $i _ { 1 } { \cdot } \mathrm { t h }$ sequence within the $g _ { 1 } \mathrm { - t h }$ group, represented as $[ \mathcal { D } _ { ( g _ { 1 } , i _ { 1 } , 1 , : ) } , . . . , \mathcal { D } _ { ( g _ { 1 } , i _ { 1 } , N _ { S } , : ) } ]$ , and analogously for $\mathbf { v } _ { g _ { 2 } , i _ { 2 } }$ . The matrix ${ \bf U } = ( u _ { i _ { 1 } , i _ { 2 } } ) ^ { - } \boldsymbol { N _ { I } } \times \boldsymbol { N _ { I } }$ encapsulates the covariance between the $i _ { 1 } { \cdot } \mathrm { t h }$ and $i _ { 2 } \mathrm { - t h }$ sequences across all distinct groups, where $\begin{array} { r } { u _ { i _ { 1 } , i _ { 2 } } = \sum _ { g _ { 1 } \neq g _ { 2 } } \mathbf { C o v } ( \mathbf { v } _ { g _ { 1 } , i _ { 1 } } , \mathbf { v } _ { g _ { 2 } , i _ { 2 } } ) . w _ { i _ { 1 } } } \end{array}$ and $w _ { i _ { 2 } }$ represent the weights of the $i _ { 1 } \ L _ { \ l }$ -th and $i _ { 2 } .$ -th sequence, respectively. The vector $\mathbf { w } = [ w _ { 1 } , \dots , w _ { N _ { I } } ]$ encompasses the weights for all sequences, with each element $w _ { i }$ corresponding to the weight of the i-th sequence.

To characterize the correlation relationships among different sequences within each group, along with their weight limitations, a normalization constraint is imposed. This normalization is crucial for bounding the solution space and preventing overfitting, and is mathematically defined as:

$$
\sum_ {i _ {1}, i _ {2}} w _ {i _ {1}} w _ {i _ {2}} \mathrm{Cov} \left(\mathbf {r} _ {i _ {1}}, \mathbf {r} _ {i _ {2}}\right) = \mathbf {w} ^ {T} \mathbf {Q} \mathbf {w} = 1\tag{5}
$$

Considering the indices $i _ { 1 }$ and $i _ { 2 }$ within the set $\{ 1 , \dots , N _ { I } \}$ $\mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 2 } }$ correspond to the $i _ { 1 } \cdot$ -th and $i _ { 2 } \cdot$ -th concatenated sequence from all groups, respectively. $\mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 1 } }$ is represented by $[ \mathcal { D } _ { ( 1 , i _ { 1 } , 1 , : ) } , \dots , \mathcal { D } _ { ( 1 , i _ { 1 } , N _ { S } , : ) } , \dots , \mathcal { D } _ { ( N _ { G } , i _ { 1 } , 1 , : ) } , \dots , \mathcal { D } _ { ( N _ { G } , i _ { 1 } , N _ { S } , : ) } ] .$ Similarly, $\mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 2 } }$ is defined in the same way. The matrix $\mathbf { Q } = ( q _ { i _ { 1 } , i _ { 2 } } ) ^ { N _ { I } ^ { - } \times N _ { I } }$ captures the covariances between the concatenated sequences $\mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 1 } }$ and $\mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 2 } } .$ , with each element $q _ { i _ { 1 } , i _ { 2 } }$ defines as $\mathrm { C o v } ( \mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 1 } } , \mathbf { r } _ { i _ { 2 } } )$

The problem is then considered as a generalized eigenvalue problem. We apply the Rayleigh-Ritz theorem to address this constrained optimization problem. This approach finds an optimal coefficient vector $\mathbf { w } _ { 1 }$ to extract the inherent intrinsic component b that maximizes inter-group correlations while being constrained by intra-group variance.

$$
\mathbf {w} _ {1} = \arg \max _ {\mathbf {w}} \frac {\mathbf {w} ^ {T} \mathbf {U} \mathbf {w}}{\mathbf {w} ^ {T} \mathbf {Q} \mathbf {w}}\tag{6}
$$

Typically, this process results in $N _ { I }$ eigenvalues, arranged in descending order. Each eigenvalue is associated with its respective eigenvector. Functionally, these eigenvectors are used as filters to generate MPS-related patterns. To construct the pattern $\mathbf { y } _ { g }$ for the g-th group, the top two eigenvectors $\big \{ \mathbf { w } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { w } _ { 2 } \big \}$ are used as filter coefficients to extract the inherent intrinsic component b. Concurrently, the remaining eigenvectors $\{ \mathbf { w } _ { 3 } , . . . , \mathbf { w } _ { N _ { I } } \}$ are used to approximate the extraction of the external dynamic component e, achieved by subtracting from a randomly selected sequence within the same group, denoted as $r a n d ( \mathbf { x } _ { g } )$ . This synthesis is represented as:

$$
\mathbf {y} _ {g} = \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {N _ {S}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {I}} \left(w _ {i, s} \mathcal {D} _ {g, i, s,:} + w _ {i, s} ^ {*} \mathcal {D} _ {g, i, s,:}\right) - r a n d (\mathbf {x} _ {g})\tag{7}
$$

Here, the vector $\mathbf { w } _ { s } = [ w _ { ( 1 , s ) } , w _ { ( 2 , s ) } , . . . , w _ { ( N _ { I } , s ) } ] \in \{ \mathbf { w } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { w } _ { 2 } \}$ is the filter coefficients to extract the intrinsic component within the s-th segment, and $\mathbf { w } _ { s } ^ { * } = [ w _ { ( 1 , s ) } ^ { * } , w _ { ( 2 , s ) } ^ { * } , . . . , w _ { ( N _ { I } , s ) } ^ { * } ] \in$ $\{ \mathbf { w } _ { 3 } , \hdots , \mathbf { w } _ { N _ { I } } \}$ is the filter coefficients for the extraction of extrinsic component within the s-th segment. With this synthesis, the resulting pattern represents both inherent and external characteristics.

The proposed method for generating MPS-related patterns $\mathbf { y } _ { \mathcal { D } }$ harmonizes intrinsic and dynamic components from various groups. Each group is weighted differently, allowing for individual calculation of both intrinsic and dynamic components. These are then adjusted using group-specific weights. The vector $\mathbf { w } _ { g , s } = [ w _ { ( g , 1 , s ) } , w _ { ( g , 2 , s ) } , . . . , w _ { ( g , N _ { I } , s ) } ] \in \{ \mathbf { w } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { w } _ { 2 } \}$ } represents the filter coefficients pertinent to the intrinsic component in the s-th segment within the g-th group. Conversely, $\mathbf { w } _ { g , s } ^ { * } =$ $[ w _ { ( g , 1 , s ) } ^ { * } , w _ { ( g , 2 , s ) } ^ { * } , . . . , w _ { ( g , N _ { I } , s ) } ^ { * } ] \in \{ \mathbf { w } _ { 3 } , . . . , \mathbf { w } _ { N _ { I } } \}$ is utilized to approximate the extraction of the external dynamic component. This extraction is achieved by subtracting a randomly selected sequence from any group, denoted as $r a n d ( \mathbf { x } _ { \mathcal { D } } )$ . The method is represented as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathbf {y} _ {\mathcal {D}} = \sum_ {g = 1} ^ {N _ {G}} o _ {g} \left(\sum_ {s = 1} ^ {N _ {S}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {I}} \big (w _ {g, i, s} \mathcal {D} _ {g, i, s,:} + w _ {g, i, s} ^ {*} \mathcal {D} _ {g, i, s,:} \big)\right) \\ - r a n d (\mathbf {x} _ {\mathcal {D}}) \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

## B. PLeS Algorithm

In this subsection, we present the PLeS algorithm, which determines the optimal payload insertion length required by packet insertion operation $F _ { I }$ to preserve the spatio-temporal characteristics of the proxy traffic. The PLeS algorithm utilizes a queue θ to gather the payload length of each packet manipulated by packet padding operation $F _ { P }$ within the manipulating downstream flow $F l o w _ { m }$ . These lengths retain the spatio-temporal characteristics of the target website. If θ is not empty, the payload length at its front is dequeued and utilized by the packet insertion $F _ { I }$ as the k-th non-MPS payload length. When $\pmb { \theta }$ is depleted, the insertion payload length, designated as the k-th non-MPS payload length vector $\bar { \boldsymbol { v ^ { k } } }$ , is computed through a combination of vectors $v _ { 1 } ^ { k } , \ v _ { 2 } ^ { k }$ , and $\boldsymbol { v } _ { 3 } ^ { k }$ . These represent the k-th non-MPS packet information from $F l o w _ { m } .$ , a randomly selected downstream flow $F l o w _ { r }$ in the WebSpare algorithm as the reference downstream flow, and their cumulative distance, denoted as $D ( k )$ , respectively.

The distance $D ( k )$ of run-length cumulative payload areas up to the k-th non-MPS packet between Flow and $F l o w _ { m }$ can be recursively calculated as follows:

$$
D (k) = D (k - 1) + S _ {r} ^ {k} - S _ {m} ^ {k - 1} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \left(S _ {r} ^ {i} - S _ {m} ^ {i - 1}\right)\tag{9}
$$

where $S _ { r } ^ { k }$ denotes the run-length cumulative payload area for the k-th non-MPS packet in $F l o w _ { r }$ , and $S _ { m } ^ { k - 1 }$ denotes run-length cumulative payload area for the $( k - 1 )$ -th non-MPS packet in $F l o w _ { m } . \mathrm { A }$ nd the cumulative payload area $S ^ { k }$ can be calculated

as follows:

$$
S ^ {k} = \varepsilon \cdot \left(c ^ {k - 1} + c ^ {k}\right) / 2\tag{10}
$$

where ε denotes the maximum payload size. The element $c ^ { k }$ belongs to the run-length cumulative payload length sequence $\mathbf { c } = [ \breve { c } ^ { 1 } , c ^ { 2 } , \dots , c ^ { T } ]$ , which sequentially accumulates the payload lengths of all packets up to the T-th non-MPS packet. Each $c ^ { k }$ is calculated recursively to ensure that the cumulative payload at point k includes the lengths of all packets up to and including the k-th non-MPS packet. The calculation of $\bar { c } ^ { k }$ is expressed as follows:

$$
c ^ {k} = c ^ {k - 1} + \sum_ {i = \Phi (k - 1) + 1} ^ {\Phi (k)} l _ {i}\tag{11}
$$

where $\Phi ( k )$ is the position mapping function, and $l _ { \Phi ( k ) }$ , the payload length for the k-th non-MPS packet, is extracted.

Then the ideal payload length $L _ { m } ^ { k }$ for the k-th non-MPS packet in the manipulated flow can be derived with the cumulative distance $D ( k )$ . Using the first-order Newton-Cotes closed formula, $L _ { m } ^ { k }$ is formulated as follows:

$$
D (k) = \varepsilon \cdot \left(2 c _ {m} ^ {k - 1} + L _ {m} ^ {k}\right) / 2\tag{12}
$$

If the above formula yields a negative value for $L _ { m } ^ { k }$ , indicating an impractical negative payload length, we set $L _ { m } ^ { k }$ to zero for feasibility. Similarly, if $\bar { L } _ { m } ^ { k }$ exceeds MPS, it is capped at MPS to ensure practicability. Consequently, $\boldsymbol { v } _ { 3 } ^ { k }$ denotes the coarsegrained length vector optimally bridging $F l o w _ { m }$ and $F l o w _ { r }$ expressed as: $v _ { 3 } ^ { k } = ( \varepsilon , \bar { L _ { m } ^ { k } } )$

The estimated payload length vector $\bar { \pmb { v } } ^ { k }$ be derived from a combination of $\bar { \boldsymbol { v _ { 1 } ^ { k } } } , \boldsymbol { v _ { 2 } ^ { k } }$ , and $\mathbf { \bar { } { \boldsymbol { v } } } _ { 3 \cdot } ^ { k } \ \boldsymbol { v } _ { 1 } ^ { k } = ( \varepsilon , F l o w _ { m } ^ { k - 1 } )$ captures the length of the $( k - 1 )$ )-th non-MPS payload in $F l o w _ { m } .$ , considering that the k-th non-MPS packet has not yet arrived. $\pmb { v } _ { 2 } ^ { k } = ( \varepsilon , F l o w _ { r } ^ { k } )$ signifies the known length of the k-th non-MPS payload in $F l o w _ { r }$ . Utilizing a weighted approach, $\bar { \pmb { v } } ^ { k }$ is computed as a linear combination of these vectors:

$$
\bar {\boldsymbol {v}} ^ {k} = \alpha_ {1} ^ {k} \boldsymbol {v} _ {1} ^ {k} + \alpha_ {2} ^ {k} \boldsymbol {v} _ {2} ^ {k} + \alpha_ {3} ^ {k} \boldsymbol {v} _ {3} ^ {k}\tag{13}
$$

where $\alpha _ { 1 } ^ { k } , \alpha _ { 2 } ^ { k }$ , and $\alpha _ { 3 } ^ { k }$ represent corresponding weights that are iteratively adjusted, and k signifies the iteration step.

Once $\bar { \boldsymbol { v } } ^ { k }$ is computed, it may not correspond to typical payload lengths seen in legitimate traffic, possibly revealing a distinctive pattern of QuicCourier. To address this, the align function adjusts the estimated payload length in the $\bar { \pmb { v } } ^ { k }$ to approximate the nearest actual payload length, denoted as $\pmb { v } ^ { k } = A l i g n ( \bar { \pmb { v } } ^ { k } )$ , from the non-MPS packets in $F l o w _ { r }$ , ensuring adherence to genuine traffic features.

After deriving $v ^ { k }$ , the next step is to fine-tune the weights $\alpha _ { 1 } ^ { k + 1 } , \alpha _ { 2 } ^ { k + 1 }$ , and $\alpha _ { 3 } ^ { k + 1 }$ for the subsequent iteration. This tuning relies on the cosine distance between the vector $v ^ { k }$ and the vectors $v _ { 1 } ^ { k } , v _ { 2 } ^ { k }$ , and $\boldsymbol { v } _ { 3 } ^ { k }$ , ensuring that the coefficients are updated in line with the latest payload length adjustments. Normalization of these coefficients scales them to a standardized range, maintaining the proportionality of their influence on the resulting payload length. ## V. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

In this section, we validate the performance of the proposed QuicCourier scheme using a real-world dataset. Due to its sustained reliability in real network conditions, our analysis primarily concentrates on its security within both the general and capability-maximizing adversarial models.

## A. Experimental Setup

1) Dataset: In response to the absence of publicly available QUIC website traffic datasets, we initiated the collection of target website traffic within a real-world network environment. This was achieved using automated web browsing scripts, which accessed designated websites at 30-second intervals. The data collection process utilized a computer based in Nanjing, China (Windows 10-64 b, 16 GB RAM). Our proxy node, a virtual private server (VPS) located in Los Angeles was configured with 1 CPU and 2 GB of memory. For the proxy protocol, we selected Socks5, known for its widespread usage. Its implementation was based on the golang language. We deployed the proxy server software on the VPS and installed the proxy client software on the local computer. This setup enabled the client to establish a proxy tunnel with the VPS and access web resources via this node. Both the sending and receiving plugins of QuicCourier were integrated into the respective server and client proxy programs. Additionally, both legitimate and covert proxy traffic data were collected and stored on the VPS.

2) Evaluation Method: The experiments are designed to evaluate the performance of the proposed covert channel under both general and capability-maximizing adversarial models. In the general adversarial model, the warden is unaware of the covert channel scheme and focuses on detecting anomalies within the network traffic. For the covert channel, the goal is to ensure that covert traffic is misclassified as legitimate and remains indistinguishable from its original categories, avoiding detection or reclassification into anomalous categories. In contrast, under the capability-maximizing adversarial model, the warden is fully aware of the covert channel scheme and has access to both legitimate and covert traffic samples. Here, the warden’s primary objective is to accurately distinguish between covert and legitimate traffic while minimizing any interference with regular communication.

Since QuicCourier is designed to mimic legitimate traffic patterns, network traffic classification tools are well-suited in both adversarial models to evaluate how effectively it blends covert traffic into legitimate flows. We utilized five typical network traffic classification tools: AppS [7], FS-Net [43], DF [44], EBSNN [45], and NeuTic [46]. AppS employs conventional machine learning, while the other four employ deep learning framework.

Furthermore, under the capability-maximizing adversarial model, four covert channel detection tools were employed to determine the presence of covert channels: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test [47], -similarity test [48], NetBeacon [49], and FlowLens [50]. The KS-test measures the maximum difference between empirical distribution functions to compare the packet length distributions. The -similarity test evaluates the temporal relationships within packet sequences by calculating the relative differences between consecutive packet lengths. NetBeacon extracts features at both the packet level (e.g., packet length, packet timing) and the flow level (e.g., mean and variance of packet lengths and inter-packet delays) and uses a Random Forest classifier for detection. FlowLens focuses on the distributions of packet lengths and inter-packet arrival times, applying quantization and feature selection techniques, and performs detection using an XGBoost classifier.

To validate the effectiveness of QuicCourier, we compare it with two variant schemes, demonstrating that its superior undetectability arises from the efficient utilization of traffic redundancy through the proposed characterization and generative modeling approach. QuicVanilla is a baseline method that does not leverage the dynamic features of traffic and instead applies fixed-length padding to each downstream packet. QuicMarkov, on the other hand, incorporates a first-order Markov transition model to capture the redundancy in website browsing traffic. It adjusts run-length values and non-MPS payload lengths based on preceding packets, utilizing a set of dynamically changing transition matrices, the utilized transition matrice strategy of which have been applied in traffic analysis and traffic obfuscation [51], [52].

These experiments were carried out on an Intel i9-12900 K CPU, 64 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 with 24 GB GPU memory. We adopted a 5-fold cross-validation method for each experiment.

3) Evaluation Metric: To accommodate the requirement of a predefined number of packets in most network traffic classification tools, we standardized the number of packets per flow to be N <sub>=</sub> 3000 in the experiments. The proposed network covert channel scheme also uses N as the available carrier packet number per flow to ensure the fairness. Unlike conventional network covert channels, which have a defined covert transmission rate, the proposed scheme embeds a fixed amount of M bytes per flow. Consequently, with each flow comprising N carrier packets, the average bytes per packet (bpp), M/N, serves as the metric for measuring the capacity of the network covert channel.

To evaluate the similarity between legitimate and covert traffic samples, the perplexity (PPL) is first utilized in our experiment. The perplexity is a commonly used metric for evaluating language model performance [53]. It is calculated based on the average logarithmic probability assigned to each word in a text. Considering that the request-response pattern in website browsing mirrors communication behavior, each packet in this scenario captures specific interaction details. Therefore, the length of each packet’s payload can be likened to a word. The perplexity can be calculated by:

$$
P P L = 2 ^ {- \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {j = 2} ^ {n} \log_ {2} p (z _ {j} | \mathbf {z} _ {1: j - 1})}\tag{14}
$$

where $\mathbf { z } = [ z _ { 1 } , z _ { 2 } , \dots , z _ { n } ]$ denotes the payload length sequence of a network flow, and $\mathbf { z } _ { 1 : j - 1 }$ indicates the sequence of payload length up to the $( j - 1 )$ -th packet. Generally, a lower perplexity value indicates a higher degree of similarity between the flows.

Furthermore, we also employed the widely used Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) as a similarity metric to assess the similarity between legitimate and covert channel samples. The calculation of JSD is as follows:

$$
J S D = \frac {1}{2} D _ {K L} \left(P _ {\alpha} \| \frac {P _ {\alpha} + P _ {\beta}}{2}\right) + \frac {1}{2} D _ {K L} \left(P _ {\beta} \| \frac {P _ {\alpha} + P _ {\beta}}{2}\right)\tag{15}
$$

where $P _ { \alpha }$ and $P _ { \beta }$ are the statistical distributions of network flow α and $\beta ,$ , respectively. $D _ { K L } ( \cdot \| \cdot )$ denotes the KL divergence.

Beyond similarity metrics, the actual results of network traffic identification tools are crucial in assessing security. We utilize three identification metrics for both the general and capabilitymaximizing adversarial models. In the general model, the Original Detection Rate (ODR) quantifies the classifier’s detection rate for original web browsing traffic. Conversely, the Secret Evasion Rate (SER) gauges the classifier’s evasion rate for covert traffic, evaluating the degree to which such traffic is categorized within its original class. The formulations for ODR and SER are delineated as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} O D R = \frac {2 \times P _ {l} \times R _ {l}}{P _ {l} + R _ {l}} \\ S E R = \frac {2 \times P _ {c} \times R _ {c}}{P _ {c} + R _ {c}} \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

whereP<sub>l</sub>andR<sub>l</sub>denote the precision and recall for the legitimate traffic, while $P _ { c }$ and $R _ { c }$ correspond to the precision and recall for the covert traffic classified as its legitimate class. A higher SER relative to ODR signifies a more effective deception of the warden.

In the capability-maximizing model, we utilize the accuracy (Acc) metric to evaluate the resistance of covert channels against 2-class traffic identification tools. A lower accuracy value is more desirable for resisting covert channel detection.

$$
A c c = \frac {T P + T N}{T P + T N + F P + F N}\tag{17}
$$

where the true positive (TP) and false negative (FN) are used to indicate correctly identified and misclassified covert traffic, respectively, while the true negative (TN) and false positive $( F P )$ pertained to the legitimate traffic. In addition, we use the Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) curve and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) to evaluate the resistance of covert channels against machine learning-based covert traffic detection tools, where the ROC provides a visual representation and the AUC measures the overall discriminative ability to distinguish covert traffic from legitimate traffic

$$

## C. Undetectability in the General Adversarial Model

In the general adversarial model, the warden functions without awareness of the proposed covert channel. The warden’s main goal is to identify any anomaly within the network traffic. This involves the continuous monitoring of all network activities using sophisticated traffic identification tools. These tools excel in recognizing different websites, even when proxies are employed to alter the traffic’s statistical properties. For the proposed QuicCourier scheme, the objective is to ensure that covert traffic samples are misclassified as part of their original categories and not marked as unusual or attributed to different categories.

To validate the effectiveness in the general adversarial model, we generated MPS-related patterns using 3,000 traffic samples for each website. In a separate set of 3,000 samples, we designated 400 for secret data transmission, used another 400 as legitimate samples, and allocated the remaining 2,200 as part of the benchmark dataset for training classifiers. This method resulted in a comprehensive training dataset totaling 22,000 samples, ensuring a balanced distribution between targeted website traffic and other traffic types. ## D. Undetectability in the Capability-Maximizing Adversarial Model

In the capability-maximizing adversarial model, where the warden possesses detailed knowledge of QuicCourier, the primary objective is to accurately differentiate between covert and legitimate traffic without impeding regular communication. Utilizing a balanced dataset comprising both legitimate and covert samples for each target website, the warden develops specialized classifiers aimed at identifying covert traffic. To optimize warden’s effectiveness, we postulate the existence of distinct classifiers for each target website, equipped with the ability to discern covert traffic based on the traffic samples.

In these experiments, 3,000 flows per website were utilized for generating MPS-related patterns. For testing purposes, an additional 3,000 flows were employed, with half designated for transmitting secret data (positive samples) and the other half serving as control (negative samples). In the two-class classification scenario, we used an 8:2 training-to-testing split, with accuracy as the primary performance metric.

For legitimate traffic, each classifier demonstrates nearrandom guess accuracy (around 50%) since it attempts to differentiate between two sets of legitimate traffic samples. To evaluate overall undetectability, we calculate the average accuracy across websites for each covert channel scheme and classifier. The “↑” symbol indicates the increase in detection accuracy for covert traffic compared to legitimate traffic. As shown in Table VII, QuicVanilla is almost entirely exposed, with four advanced classifiers detecting its covert traffic with over 95% accuracy. However, because QuicVanilla’s inherent padding does not disrupt packet direction sequences, it remains indistinguishable under the DF classifier. In contrast, QuicMarkov performs significantly better, showing an average 20% reduction in detection accuracy compared to QuicVanilla. However, as the bpp value increases, the detection rates for QuicMarkov also rise, as its first-order Markov model fails to capture longer-dependency spatio-temporal characteristics required to conceal larger amounts of secret data effectively. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating covert channel schemes across multiple classifiers to comprehensively assess their robustness.

On the other hand, QuicCourier demonstrates substantial declines in detection accuracy, approaching random chance predictions. For instance, AppS achieves a maximum accuracy of only 55.2%, indicating its inability to detect QuicCourier. Deep learning-based classifiers such as DF, FS-Net, and EBSNN perform slightly better, with average detection accuracies near 55%. Specifically, DF achieves the highest accuracy with websites like ACM and CGsociety, FS-Net performs better with ACM and Xhs, and EBSNN performs best with Facebook. Notably, NeuTic delivers the strongest detection performance, particularly for ACM and Facebook traffic, achieving an average accuracy of approximately 60% when the average secret data transmitted per browsing session is around 12 KB. However, it is important to note that NeuTic incurs significantly higher computational costs compared to other classifiers.

## E. Discussion

Experimental results under different adversarial models demonstrate that detecting QuicCourier remains a significant challenge for wardens, even in the capability-maximizing adversarial model. In this scenario, wardens are equipped with labeled covert traffic samples and advanced detection tools, yet their ability to accurately identify QuicCourier traffic remains limited. Despite these promising results, QuicCourier faces two primary limitations when deployed in real-world Internet environments.

The first limitation lies in adapting to the heterogeneous and dynamic statistical redundancy of traffic across different websites. In open-world scenarios, websites exhibit substantial heterogeneity, resulting in significant variations in the statistical characteristics of their traffic. Additionally, traffic patterns are highly dynamic and vary widely across websites, requiring individualized modeling of traffic patterns for each target website to construct effective covert channels. Experimental results indicate that websites with highly dynamic content offer significantly greater secure embedding capacities compared to those with static content. However, accurately assessing the secure capacity of each website remains inherently challenging.

In practical applications, this limitation necessitates prioritizing websites with dynamic traffic patterns to ensure secure covert channels while reducing covert transmission rates for static websites. Furthermore, even for the same website, variations in traffic characteristics caused by content updates or access configurations complicate modeling. Addressing this requires collecting more initial traffic samples and continuously updating datasets to maintain accurate and comprehensive traffic models. Thus, adapting to the heterogeneity and dynamic nature of website traffic redundancy remains a core challenge for QuicCourier.

The second limitation is the reliance on web browsing behavior, which inherently requires user involvement. The establishment of the proposed covert channel depends on web browsing activities, necessitating some level of user participation in real-world applications. Secret data is transmitted by embedding message fragments into individual network flows, requiring users to actively browse websites.

Mitigating this limitation involves selecting websites with high-frequency automatic refresh mechanisms, which are already common among many popular websites. Such mechanisms significantly reduce the need for manual clicks or refreshes during covert communication. Additionally, browserbased automatic refresh configurations can be utilized to further minimize user involvement. These strategies help alleviate the dependence on user behavior, making the proposed scheme more practical for real-world deployment.

Despite these limitations, QuicCourier’s security is bolstered by the unpredictable and complex nature of the Internet. First, the vastness of the Internet makes exhaustive monitoring by wardens impractical [54]. Many websites accessed by Quic-Courier are likely to evade detection due to the sheer scale of the network. Experimental results under the general adversarial model confirm the inherent challenges wardens face in detecting QuicCourier under typical scenarios. Second, the problem of data imbalance presents an additional hurdle [55]. In real-world scenarios, it is unlikely that wardens can gather a comprehensive dataset with sufficient samples of QuicCourier traffic. As QuicCourier’s usage increases, its traffic will blend more seamlessly with legitimate traffic, contaminating detection datasets and further complicating detection efforts. This scenario adds significant complexity to the warden’s task in an uncontrolled, real-world environment, in sharp contrast to laboratory conditions. By leveraging the Internet’s scale and unpredictability, QuicCourier gains additional layers of security, making it a robust and practical solution for covert communication.

Beyond the widespread adoption ofthe QUIC protocol, which offers a vast range of potential carriers, QuicCourier’s effectiveness lies in its innovative utilization of spatio-temporal statistical redundancy inherent in QUIC web browsing traffic. By employing a characterization generative model for web resource patterns, it ensures consistency with legitimate traffic, even against advanced statistical detection techniques. Furthermore, the extensive state information within QUIC’s encrypted structure minimizes the risk of detectable protocol anomalies. In contrast, covert channels built on other protocols often fail to preserve behavioral consistency. For instance, WEASEL embeds secret data into DNS elements such as domain names, but these domain names are non-existent in DNS records and deviate from legitimate linguistic patterns, exhibiting unnatural character distributions that make them vulnerable to statistical detection [56]. Similarly, IPv6teal embeds data in the Flow Label field of the IPv6 header, but frequent and inconsistent modifications across packets disrupt normal IPv6 traffic patterns, introducing detectable anomalies [57]. Moreover, once these covert channels are identified, simple countermeasures such as traffic normalization can effectively neutralize them without affecting legitimate communications. By contrast, QuicCourier’s strategic use ofredundancy, combined with its ability to maintain comprehensive behavioral consistency, significantly enhances its stealth and resilience, distinguishing it from traditional covert channels.

## VI. CONCLUSION

In this study, we introduce QuicCourier, a novel covert channel that utilizes the dynamic spatio-temporal characteristics of QUIC-web browsing traffic for secret data transmission. This method manipulates QUIC packets within proxy services while maintaining standard proxy communications. The inherent encapsulation capabilities of proxy services further support such manipulations. QuicCourier exhibits robustness against various traffic analysis tools under both general and capabilitymaximizing adversarial models. This covert channel framework offers a way to seamlessly integrate various web services with web proxies, enabling high-capacity covert communications in real networks.

This work represents an initial exploration into leveraging proxy services and traffic redundancy for covert communication, currently focusing on web browsing behavior. While our implementation demonstrates the effectiveness of utilizing statistical redundancy in web traffic, similar redundancy patterns also exist in other network activities, such as mobile applications and streaming multimedia. These areas suggest promising directions for future research, expanding the applicability of the proposed method beyond web browsing.
