# Is the Data Really Noisy? Rethinking Traffic Filtering for Encrypted Traffic Classification

Chungang Lin, Weiyao Zhang, Meng Shen, Yilong Jiang, Tianyu Zuo, Haitong Luo Yequan Wang, Xuying Meng, and Yujun Zhang

Abstract—Encrypted traffic classification, which is essential for network security, has long relied on traffic filtering as a preprocessing routine to eliminate disturbances from noisy data. While such filtering—often based on coarse criteria (e.g., protocol, length)—has become a de facto choice, its insufficiently understood impact renders the resulting performance improvements questionable. This paper aims to understand and mitigate the impact of traffic filtering for encrypted traffic classification. Through a comprehensive study covering phase, category, and strategy perspectives, we demonstrate the broadly negative impact of traffic filtering. In particular, although traffic filtering appears to improve overall classification performance, it simultaneously causes severe performance degradation on numerous (>35%) traffic categories—a trade-off observed across diverse filtering strategies. We further reveal that the primary cause lies in data-driven factors, including the compromise of traffic fidelity and the inevitable removal of valuable data. To mitigate the negative impact of traffic filtering, we explore the filtering-free framework for encrypted traffic classification and present TraCare, which enhances model understanding of noisy data rather than filtering it. Specifically, TraCare enhances the model’s understanding of task-specific and general traffic by capturing behavioral relevance through an activity-aware enhancement module and a self-built general traffic corpus, respectively. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the filtering-free framework, with TraCare achieving up to 11.37% relative improvement in fine-grained classification performance and reducing the negative impacts across different traffic categories by 87% to 94%. Moreover, TraCare demonstrates high deployment feasibility, requiring 90% fewer labels and achieving 6.82 times faster traffic classification. The code and corpus are available at https://github.com/lincgcg/TraCare-ICT.

Index Terms—Encrypted traffic classification, traffic filtering, measurement study.

With the widespread adoption of privacy-enhanced encryption techniques, such as VPN and Tor, encrypted traffic classification has become essential for network security. For instance, identifying malware applications from encrypted traffic enables hosts to alert users, mitigating risks of information leakage and financial loss [9]. Traditional rule-based methods [10], [11], that extract basic attributes, are ineffective for classifying encrypted traffic. In contrast, recent advances in machine learning [5], [6], [12], [13], particularly the adoption of pre-trained models [14], [3], [1], have enhanced the effectiveness of encrypted traffic classification by enabling the automatic extraction of valuable features from raw traffic.

As a preliminary step to the encrypted traffic classification model, traffic filtering is employed during both the model development and deployment phases to avoid disturbance from noisy data [14], [3], [5], [6], [13], [12]. Based on an intuitive assumption that some protocols are task-irrelevant and short-length flows/packets are less informative, many studies remove packets with specific protocols and those with short flow/packet lengths. For instance, before model training, FP [12] removes all packets that do not use TCP or UDP protocols. FS-Net [6] filters out flows with lengths less than two. In contrast, recent pre-trained encrypted traffic classification models [14], [3] incorporate the pre-training phase without filtering traffic, aiming to maintain traffic fidelity and facilitate learning from unlabeled data. Nonetheless, these pre-trained models still conduct traffic filtering to eliminate noisy data during the model development (also called fine-tuning) phase. For example, ET-BERT [14] removes packets of 11 protocols, and abandons packets with data frame lengths under 80 bytes.

Although encrypted traffic classification models achieve great performance in diverse security tasks, existing filtering strategies are rough and indiscriminate. Ignoring the diversity of intrinsic behaviors of data from different tasks, traffic filtering can remove large amounts of “noisy” data by similar filtering strategies. Such extensive eliminations, coupled with a vague definition of “noisy” data, cast doubt on the efficacy of traffic filtering. On the one hand, with large-scale elimination and highly concentrated traffic, the overall classification performance becomes unconvincing. On the other hand, traffic filtering may inadvertently remove non-noisy data, resulting in different impacts on the model’s learning ability for individual traffic categories. For example, some works [14], [15] consider DHCP packets irrelevant to the transmission content, and roughly filter them. However, OWL [16] highlights that multicast and broadcast packets, such as DHCP and LLMNR, provide valuable information about IoT devices—including manufacturer, type, and operating system—which are essential for security tasks like IoT attack identification. Thus, rethinking the impact of traffic filtering for encrypted traffic classification is highly demanded.

This paper presents the first study on this unexplored problem, demystifying traffic filtering for traffic classification, understanding its impacts, and mitigating such impacts. Our study aims to answer the following three questions:

(1) Observation: What impact does traffic filtering have on encrypted traffic classification?

(2) Analysis: Why does traffic filtering cause these impacts?

(3) Mitigation: How to mitigate these impacts?

Observation: As shown in Fig. 1, encrypted traffic classification mainly includes two phases, where in each phase, network experts monitor traffic category distributions and apply traffic filtering strategies. To comprehensively cover the impact scopes of traffic filtering, we delve into these three levels, i.e., phase, category, and strategy. Using two representative pre-trained models [14], [3], we observe that employing inconsistent filtering across model phases brings significant performance degradation. Consistent filtering also disproportionately degrades model performance for certain traffic categories. Moreover, experiments with various filtering strategies show that different strategies can lead to varying performance changes across traffic categories, indicating that formulating a generally effective strategy remains challenging. To conclude, these results reveal the broadly negative impact of traffic filtering for encrypted traffic classification.

Analysis: To provide further understanding, we conduct indepth analyses of the reasons behind these three-level impacts. Across model phases, we compare data distributions before and after traffic filtering and find that inconsistent filtering distorts traffic fidelity, causing distribution shifts and degrading performance. Across traffic categories, we visualize the category distributions of traffic modeling results before and after filtering, and discover that filtering can inadvertently remove valuable data, leading to category-specific performance degradation. Across filtering strategies, we notice that each protocol- or length-based filtering strategy may simultaneously remove noisy and valuable data within the same task, making such removal inevitable. To conclude, the negative impacts of traffic filtering primarily result from the compromise of traffic fidelity and the inevitable removal of valuable data.

Mitigation: To preserve traffic fidelity and prevent the removal of valuable data, we explore the filtering-free framework for encrypted traffic classification. Specifically, we present TraCare to enhance the model’s ability to capture behavioral relevance between noisy data and other traffic. TraCare includes an activity-aware enhancement module that captures task-specific behavioral relevance and a self-built general traffic corpus that supports learning behavioral relevance among general traffic, thereby improving the generalization of traffic modeling. Experimental results show that TraCare outperforms SoTA pre-trained models across four tasks, with relative performance improvements of up to 11.37%. TraCare reduces the proportion of traffic categories experiencing performance degradation by 87% to 94% compared with traffic filtering. Compared with five classic enhancement methods, which fail on noisy data and even degrade performance, our activityaware enhancement module achieves stable improvements. Moreover, we evaluate TraCare’s feasibility in resourceconstrained scenarios. These results show that TraCare reduces label usage by 90% and achieves 6.82 times faster traffic classification without sacrificing performance.

In summary, the contributions of this paper are four-fold:

• To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to conduct a comprehensive and systematic study to identify the impact of traffic filtering for encrypted traffic classification.

• By in-depth analyses, we conclude that traffic filtering has a broadly negative impact for encrypted traffic classification due to its adverse impacts on traffic fidelity and its inevitable removal of valuable data.

• We explore the filtering-free framework for encrypted traffic classification and present TraCare to enhance the model’s ability to capture behavioral relevance between noisy data and other traffic data.

• We conduct extensive experiments to validate the effectiveness of the filtering-free framework, showing that TraCare outperforms SoTA models and achieves high deployment feasibility with a 90% reduction in labels and 6.82 times faster traffic classification.

## II. RELATED WORK

## A. Encrypted Traffic Classification

Encrypted traffic classification has attracted increasing attention in both academia and industry due to its critical role in network security and management. In the industry, security companies such as ENEA [17], TrafficWiz [18], Allot [19], and LiveAction [20] are actively developing encrypted traffic classification technologies. For instance, LiveAction [20] applies machine learning to analyze encrypted traffic, supporting threat detection and network monitoring without requiring decryption. In academic research, existing methods can primarily be classified into machine learning models [5], [6], [13], [12], [4], [21], [22], [23], [24] and pre-trained ones [14], [3], [1], [25], [26], [27], [28]. Beyond developing specific classification models, recent studies [1], [29], [30] have also explored various enhancement methods to improve the understanding of encrypted traffic.

Machine Learning and Pre-trained Models. Machine learning models [5], [6], [31], [32], [12], [33], [34] achieve effective classification through manual or automatic feature extraction. For instance, FP [12] extracts statistical features in flows, using clustering and cross-correlation for encrypted traffic classification. FS-Net [6] utilizes RNNs to derive data representations from packet length sequences. However, these models require large amounts of labeled data, which is impractical in the real world [35], [36], [37], [27], [38], [26]. In contrast, pre-trained models introduce the pre-training and fine-tuning paradigm, including self-supervised pre-training on a vast corpus of unlabeled data, followed by supervised fine-tuning on smallscale task-specific labeled data. A notable example is ET-BERT [14], which incorporates new encryption protocol data into its corpus, utilizing a BERT [39]-based model for various security tasks. Besides, YaTC [3] models network traffic as images and applies an MAE [40]-based model to discern flowlevel traffic relationships. This new paradigm offers a solution to the dependency on large-scale labeled data, and has become an effective and growing trend for network security [1].

Enhancement Methods. Different from the above-mentioned design of a specific model, the enhancement methods [41], [1], [29], [30] incorporate specific modules (e.g., contrastive learning, representation enhancement) into the existing model to improve its understanding of network traffic. Rosetta [29] focuses on enhancing the model’s understanding of TCP semantics through three data enhancement methods based on TCP mechanisms. TrafficFormer [1] introduces Random Initialization Field Augmentation (RIFA) to mitigate overreliance on non-informative header fields. NetGPT [35] employs Shuffling Header Field (SHF) to capture the semantic invariance of these fields. Chen et al. [30] argue that single-flow classification causes repetitive results. Hence, they propose d-CV to enhance model classification performance by aggregating the results from multiple flows. However, they do not explicitly consider the presence of noisy data, which can even result in negative impacts on classification performance. Our work differs from these methods, as we focus on how to capture behavioral relevance and enhance the model’s understanding of noisy data from task-specific and general traffic.

## B. Traffic Filtering

The fundamental objective of traffic filtering is to improve the quality of data by removing noisy data, thereby ultimately enhancing model classification performance [3], [5], [6], [39], [42]. As RFC 5475 states, “The motivation for Filtering is to remove all packets that are not of interest.” [43]. Specifically, traffic filtering operates by checking packet attributes against predefined filtering strategies and retaining only the matching ones. In academic research, traffic filtering strategies generally fall into two classes: removing short flows/packets and removing packets from task-irrelevant protocols.

Removing Short Flows/Packets. The filtering strategy of removing short packets/flows is the main strategy adopted by some methods [14], [6] based on sequence data. These methods treat data of short length as noisy data that hinders model learning. For example, Liu et al. [6] remove flows shorter than two packets. Lin et al. [14] argue that packets with lengths less than 80 bytes are typically non-actual communication packets. Therefore, they remove these irrelevant packets to help the model focus on the specific traffic. However, short packets/flows represent different traffic behaviors (e.g., web page requests) than long packets/flows. The current rough filtering strategies cause the model to ignore these traffic behaviors, thereby limiting the model’s learning ability.

Removing Packets from Task-irrelevant Protocols. Another class of traffic filtering strategy is to remove packets from task-irrelevant protocols [14], [3], [5], [35]. Some methods [14], [3], [5] treat data from protocols that are not related to specific tasks as noisy data and remove these noisy data. Lin et al. [14] employ a filtering strategy of removing all packets in specific protocols (e.g., DHCP and DNS). Similarly, Xiao et al. [5] remove all packets of ARP, ICMP, and DNS protocols. However, data from the same protocol can represent different traffic behaviors. For example, DNS protocol data might indicate a typical domain name resolution process or, alternatively, a DNS amplification attack. Therefore, the current rough strategy of removing noisy data based on protocols can hinder the model from learning specific traffic behaviors.

## A. Impact Scope of Traffic Filtering

The first step in exploring the impact is to clarify the relationship between traffic filtering and traffic classification. Specifically, encrypted traffic classification mainly includes two phases: the model development phase and the model deployment phase [11], [44], [45]. During the model development phase, the primary goal is to construct an encrypted traffic classification model. Upon completion of model development, the focus shifts to the model deployment phase, where the model is applied to classify newly emerging encrypted traffic accurately. As shown in Fig. 1, network experts monitor the distribution of traffic categories in both phases and design corresponding traffic filtering strategies. These strategies are applied in the process of transmitting raw traffic data to different model phases, ultimately improving model performance on specific tasks by removing noisy data.

Based on the relationship between traffic filtering and encrypted traffic classification, we can identify that traffic filtering impacts traffic classification at three levels: phase, category, and strategy. Thus, in §III-C to §III-E, we uncover the impact of traffic filtering by answering the following three questions: 1) Phase-level impact: What impact does traffic filtering at different model phases (i.e., development and deployment) have on final classification performance? 2) Category-level impact: What impact does traffic filtering have on the classification performance of the model on different traffic categories? 3) Strategy-level impact: What impact do different traffic filtering strategies (formulated by network experts) have on model performance?

To comprehensively measure the impact of traffic filtering, we inevitably include some expected observations. Nevertheless, these observations provide necessary perspectives for a comprehensive understanding of traffic filtering. Beyond these expected observations, we also reveal many unexpected observations, e.g., the broadly negative impacts of traffic filtering from the three perspectives.

The conclusions drawn from our measurement study are not limited to these two models. As analyzed in §III-F, the impacts of traffic filtering are fundamentally data-driven rather than model-specific, ensuring their generalizability across a broader range of models. To further confirm this generalizability, we extend the analysis to other models in $\ S \mathrm { V - C } ,$ demonstrating that the observed impacts of traffic filtering remain consistent across diverse model types.

## B. Selection of Models and Tasks

2) Task Selection: To enable fine-grained analyses, we focus on multi-class tasks rather than two-class detection tasks. We adopt three security tasks (Tasks 1-3) from the original studies (i.e., ET-BERT [14] and YaTC [3]) to maintain consistency with the filtering strategies used in these works. We also introduce a new task (Task 4) to assess the generalizability of the filtering strategies beyond the original settings. Application Classification (Task 1) and Service Identification (Task 2) involve classifying encrypted VPN and Tor traffic on the ISCX-VPN [46] and ISCX-Tor [47] datasets. In detail, Task 1 aims to identify the specific application generating the traffic (e.g., FTPS and SFTP), while Task 2 focuses on recognizing the broader service category behind the application (e.g., File Transfer). Malware Classification (Task 3) focuses on categorizing encrypted traffic into different malware families. The USTC-TFC [48] dataset contains 10 normal software and 10 malware from infrastructures like smart grids. We focus on fine-grained malware application classification. IoT Attack Identification (Task 4) aims to identify the type of IoT device targeted by specific attack types on the CIC-IoT [49] dataset. For example, the simcamTCPFlood category indicates that a smart camera is under a TCP flood attack.

To establish observations and analyses with broad generalizability beyond specific cases, we make the following choices.

Our selected datasets comprehensively cover encrypted traffic from the three most important uses of encryption on the Internet—VPN, Tor, and TLS [50].

1) Model Selection: To study the impact of traffic filtering and prevent bias of low classification performance, we first focus on pre-trained models that can achieve state-of-the-art performance, i.e., ET-BERT [14] and YaTC [3]. ET-BERT converts byte sequences into word-like sequences, enabling direct pre-training on a BERT-based model. Similarly, YaTC models network flows as images, leveraging masked image modeling from the computer vision domain for pre-training.

## C. Impact at Different Model Phases

1) Experiment Setup: To explore the impacts of traffic filtering at different model phases, we create four scenarios based on whether traffic filtering is employed at specific model phases, as shown in TABLE II. As the development and deployment phases correspond to model training and testing [11], we conceptualize the phase employing traffic filtering as a dataset processed by traffic filtering. Following existing pretrained traffic classification models [14], [3], [1], we adopt a flow-level random data split. Specifically, for each traffic category, we randomly select at most 5,000 flows and then split the selected flows into training, validation, and testing sets with a ratio of 8:1:1. We adopt this setting to keep the experimental setting consistent with prior works and to avoid introducing additional confounding factors when isolating the impact of traffic filtering. To prevent the randomness caused by random sampling, we perform statistical analyses. Specifically, we obtain 20 sampled datasets by setting different random seeds and report the confidence interval of results at a 95% confidence level. ## F. Understanding Different Impacts

1) Analysis for Phase-level Impact: To understand the cause of phase-level impact, we analyze traffic fidelity to assess data distributions before and after traffic filtering. Traffic fidelity is a key metric in network tasks [55], [56]. It refers to whether processed traffic preserves critical statistical properties (e.g., flow size distribution) of the raw traffic after operations like traffic filtering. However, traffic filtering roughly processes the raw traffic by protocols and lengths without caring about the deep characteristics, which can compromise the fidelity of the traffic data to a certain extent. Common metrics for measuring traffic fidelity include flow size and trace size [55]. Therefore, in Fig. 6, we plot the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of flow size and trace size to compare the data distributions before and after filtering. The results show that the traffic-filtered data cannot maintain a distribution similar to the raw traffic. Overall, these differences in distribution make the model’s learned data distribution inconsistent, leading to performance degradation.

Cause of Phase-level Impact (C1): Inconsistent traffic filtering compromises traffic fidelity, resulting in a varied data distribution learned by the model at different model phases, ultimately causing performance degradation.

2) Analysis for Category-level Impact: To better analyze the impact on different traffic categories, we visualize the distribution of model outputs for different categories before and after traffic filtering. Specifically, we apply t-SNE [57] to project high-dimensional feature vectors extracted by ET-BERT into 2D vectors. The results in Fig. 7 show that the feature vector distribution of some traffic categories changes significantly when traffic filtering is employed, such as scp and AIM. Given that traffic filtering essentially removes data, we argue that traffic filtering inadvertently removes valuable data while removing noisy data. Here, valuable data refers to data that is beneficial for the model’s learning of specific traffic categories. The removal of such valuable data leads to significant changes in feature vector distributions, which degrade model performance on some traffic categories.

![](images/c99e3ddf502615a352d880fd76ae57b83d7495f0d2f48cc1100a683450605215.jpg)

Taking the scp and AIM categories as examples. Before traffic filtering (left side of Fig. 7), the feature vector of scp is highly dispersed, indicating difficulty for the model in classification. In contrast, the feature vector of AIM clusters more tightly, which represents good classification performance. However, the situation is reversed after traffic filtering (right side of Fig. 7). The scp data distribution becomes tight, while the AIM data distribution is scattered. We conclude that traffic filtering enhances the effectiveness of the model in learning partial data (e.g., scp) by removing noisy data. Nonetheless, the current rough traffic filtering inadvertently removes valuable data for model learning, causing the model to be hindered in learning other categories (e.g., AIM).

Cause of Category-level Impact (C2): Traffic filtering can inadvertently remove data that is critical for the model’s learning on some traffic categories, causing a degradation in model performance for these traffic categories.

3) Analysis for Strategy-level Impact: We further analyze the heatmap results in Fig. 4 and find that the judgment of data as valuable or noisy depends on its behavioral relevance to specific traffic categories. Specifically, behavioral relevance means that data is related to a specific traffic category in terms of traffic behaviors (e.g., domain name resolution). We take the DNS protocol data as an example. Specifically, for the youtube, netflix, ftps, and sftp categories, Fig. 3 shows that the DNS atomic strategy removes 0.31%, 0.03%, 0.01%, and 0.01% of their traffic data, respectively. Although the removed volume is relatively small, the resulting performance changes are category-dependent. This indicates that the impact of removing DNS traffic is not determined solely by the amount of removed data, but also by the behavioral role of DNS traffic in different traffic categories. For video streaming services like youtube and netflix, data generated through domain name resolution provides valuable information, such as specific domain names for different services, enabling accurate model identification of these categories. In contrast, DNS is considered noisy when identifying traffic categories for secure file transfers, such as ftps and sftp, due to its lack of primary relevance to file transfer. Thus, the atomic strategy of removing DNS protocol data results in performance decreases for youtube and netflix categories while enhancing model performance for ftps and sftp categories.

The results demonstrate the connection between data’s dual role (noisy or valuable) and its behavioral relevance. Considering that data of the same type (e.g., protocols and length limits) can be noisy or valuable for different categories, we conclude that absolutely noisy data is hard to detect and remove, and removal of valuable data is inevitable when employing traffic filtering. This inevitability results in traffic filtering’s inability to improve the model’s performance on all traffic categories with these filtering strategies.

Cause of Strategy-level Impact (C3): Data of the same type (e.g., protocols and length limits) act as noisy or valuable data for different traffic categories based on their inherent behaviors. Thus, filtering strategies based on type inevitably result in the removal of valuable data.

## IV. TRACARE

Next, we introduce three high-level insights to answer how to mitigate these negative impacts in §IV-A. To synthesize those insights, the design of TraCare is provided in §IV-B.

## A. High-level Insights

Insight #1: We explore the filtering-free framework for encrypted traffic classification, as a new paradigm to mitigate the compromise of traffic fidelity and the inevitable removal of valuable data, both resulting from traffic filtering.

As we saw earlier, inconsistent traffic filtering compromises network traffic fidelity, significantly degrading model performance (C1). Under consistent traffic filtering, we observe that traffic filtering degrades the model’s performance on over 35% of traffic categories. The root cause is the inadvertent removal of valuable data when removing noisy data (C2). Moreover, we find that “inadvertent removal of valuable data” is inevitable, and it is infeasible to formulate an optimal filtering strategy to remove absolutely noisy data. Given that data of the same type can act as both noisy and valuable data depending on its inherent behavior, this duality inevitably results in removing valuable data during traffic filtering (C3).

To preserve traffic fidelity and avoid the removal of valuable data, we explore the filtering-free framework as a new paradigm for encrypted traffic classification, which also reduces the human and material consumption of strategy formulation and data processing. However, traffic filtering aims to eliminate disturbances from noisy data. We may receive too much noisy data without traffic filtering, making model training more difficult. In the following (Insight #2 and #3), we address this problem based on the behavioral relevance between noisy and other traffic data revealed in §III-F.

Insight #2: We argue that noisy data may not offer explicit value for model learning but retain inherent behavioral relevance to other data from the same activity, and develop an activity-aware enhancement module to improve the model’s understanding of task-specific noisy data.

![](images/713a966dfb80e1f5e98ce07128e507e343d989cc178d978229379b5aae5abb95.jpg)  
Fig. 8: Activity-aware enhancement module of TraCare.

The noisy data problem implies that during model training, the model is exposed to data that does not contribute to learning, thereby hindering its ability to extract valuable information [14], [35], [58]. Consequently, this may result in reduced classification performance and weakened generalization ability [58]. However, the cause C3 in §III-F3 indicate that data can act as either noisy data or valuable data based on its intrinsic behaviors. These different behaviors constitute the communication activities of a specific traffic category [59], [60]. Therefore, establishing activity-level relationships enables the model to better understand these intrinsic behaviors and connect noisy data with other relevant data, thereby mitigating the negative impact of noisy data.

A straightforward solution to learning activity-level relationships is to predict whether different data originate from the same trace during the pre-training phase, where a trace records network communication activities over a period of time [55]. This method draws inspiration from ET-BERT [14]. They use a same-origin burst prediction pre-training task to establish burst-level relationships. Specifically, ET-BERT employs a binary classifier to predict whether different data come from the same burst during the pre-training phase, thus effectively capturing and learning burst-level relationships. However, introducing a new pre-training task significantly increases computational overhead. Additionally, these pretraining tasks are architecture-specific and cannot be easily transferred to pre-trained models with different architectures.

To address the aforementioned limitations, we develop an activity-aware enhancement module that leverages contrastive learning to mitigate the negative impact of noisy data on specific tasks. To enhance the model’s understanding of task-specific noisy data, this module manages to reduce the distance between data from the same activity in the latent space. This is based on the observation that highly correlated data (e.g., data from the same activity) tend to cluster closely in the latent space [36], [61], [62]. As shown in Fig. 8, before model optimization, this module performs the same activity judgment and flow vectorization. For the same activity judgment, inspired by graph propagation [59], we conduct the unsupervised session aggregation $f _ { \mathrm { A g g } }$ to obtain data from the same activity SA(·). In detail, we extract representative traffic features from each sample, including destination, certificate, and domain features, and apply unsupervised clustering [63] to aggregate sessions, aiming at maximizing the similarity between samples associated with the same activity.

The encoder obtains the representation of network traffic, while the activityaware layer converts the high-dimensional representation into a latent embedding space that is more conducive to identifying and understanding activity-level relationships. Here, we use $x _ { i }$ to denote the current data sample. The encoder outputs a representation $h _ { i } = \varphi _ { \alpha } ( x _ { i } )$ , and the activity-aware layer outputs a representation $z _ { i } = \psi _ { \alpha } ( h _ { i } )$ ). Inspired by contrastive learning in other fields [62], [61], [64], given an input $x _ { i } ,$ we optimize the model by the loss function:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\alpha} (i) = - \left(\frac {1}{| S A (x _ {i}) |} \sum_ {x ^ {+} \in S A (x _ {i})} \frac {\langle z _ {i} , z ^ {+} \rangle}{\tau} - \frac {1}{| X _ {- i} ^ {B} |} \sum_ {x _ {a} \in X _ {- i} ^ {B}} \frac {\langle z _ {i} , z _ {a} \rangle}{\tau}\right)\tag{1}
$$

where $X ^ { B } = \{ x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \ldots , x _ { B } \}$ denotes the set of B samples in the current batch used for contrastive learning, and $X _ { - i } ^ { B } =$ $X ^ { B } \setminus \{ x _ { i } \}$ denotes all samples in this mini-batch except the anchor sample $x _ { i } . \ S A ( x _ { i } )$ denotes the set of samples associated with the same activity as $x _ { i } .$ which is obtained by the unsupervised session aggregation $f _ { \mathrm { A g g } } . \ x ^ { + }$ denotes a positive sample from $S A ( x _ { i } )$ , and $x _ { a }$ denotes another sample from $X _ { - i } ^ { B }$ . The corresponding representations are $z ^ { + } = \psi _ { \alpha } ( \varphi _ { \alpha } ( x ^ { + } ) )$ and $z _ { a } = \psi _ { \alpha } ( \varphi _ { \alpha } ( x _ { a } ) )$ . ⟨·,·⟩ denotes the inner product between normalized representations, and the temperature coefficient τ controls the degree of attention to hard samples.

Insight #3: We argue that effective handling of noisy data requires comprehensive understanding across diverse traffic behaviors, which motivates the construction ofa general traffic corpus to enhance model generalization.

The effectiveness of the activity-aware enhancement is also determined by the encoder from the pre-training phase, where the pre-trained encoder learns to capture traffic relevance from general traffic and produce adequate general traffic representations. An inadequately general representation may lead to inappropriate model enhancements [61], [64]. Such inadequacy stems from the model’s inability to sufficiently learn general traffic data (e.g., potential noisy data). Recent studies [14], [3], [35] point out that pre-trained models can achieve a general traffic representation through self-supervised learning, enabling them to understand various traffic behaviors. However, the scope of the current pre-training corpus is too narrow, limiting the model’s capacity to thoroughly learn diverse network traffic behaviors. For instance, ET-BERT [14] leverages two public datasets and 15GB of proprietary traffic data for its 30GB pre-training corpus; and the corpus of YaTC [3] only includes 20,517 flows from four public datasets. Furthermore, the cause C2 in §III-F2 indicate that even the inadvertent removal of valuable data can significantly degrade model performance, which further emphasizes the critical need for comprehensive training data.

In response, we build a large general traffic corpus, aiming to provide a comprehensive understanding of different traffic behaviors. The three public datasets (ISCX-VPN [46], ISCX-Tor [47], and USTC-TFC [48]) contain traffic data from different applications in different environments, which can well support the model in learning the specific behaviors generated by different applications. Moreover, we build the PrivII dataset to capture various specific behaviors with different protocols. ## B. Design of TraCare

Combining the key insights above, we present TraCare, a filtering-free framework for encrypted traffic classification. Unlike existing works that design dedicated pre-trained models for general traffic representations, TraCare aims to mitigate the negative impacts of traffic filtering. Moreover, TraCare is a model-agnostic traffic classification framework and can be applied to different pre-trained models, including existing open-source models such as ET-BERT [14] and YaTC [3].

The overview of TraCare is presented in Fig. 9. Specifically, in the model pre-training phase, we build a general traffic corpus for self-supervised pre-training and obtain the pre-trained model. In the model development phase, we propose the activity-aware enhancement module to enhance the pre-trained model’s understanding of task-specific traffic using unlabeled task-specific data. The enhanced model is then equipped with a classification head and fine-tuned with limited labeled data. In the model deployment phase, the fine-tuned model directly classifies real-world traffic without traffic filtering.

Model Pre-training Phase: (Insight #1,#3) To enhance the model’s generalization to potentially noisy data, we pre-train it on the self-built general traffic corpus. Specifically, we first select the required model configuration, generate the model input for pre-training, and finally obtain the pre-trained model. Model Development Phase: (Insight #1,#2) We develop the model for specific tasks through two key modules: activityaware enhancement and few-shot fine-tuning. The activityaware enhancement module exploits the behavioral relevance between noisy data and other traffic from the same activity, enabling the model to extract traffic representations from task-specific noisy data. Subsequently, few-shot fine-tuning is performed to adapt the enhanced model for specific tasks.

Model Deployment Phase: (Insight #1) The fine-tuned model performs inference on real encrypted traffic without applying any filtering strategies, thereby preserving traffic fidelity and avoiding the removal of valuable data.

Our evaluation aims to answer the three research questions: RQ1. Can TraCare outperform state-of-the-art (SoTA) encrypted traffic classification models, without great performance degradation on each traffic category?

RQ2. What benefits can we get from individual insights? i.e., (RQ2.1) filtering-free framework, (RQ2.2) general traffic corpus, and (RQ2.3) activity-aware enhancement.

RQ3. When TraCare is deployed, how do resource constraints, task inconsistency, and data scarcity affect its feasibility?

## A. Experimental Setup

Baselines. We select seven encrypted traffic classification models as baselines, including both machine learning models and pre-trained ones. In detail, we have (1) five machine learning models, including two traditional machine learning ones (i.e., FP [12] and AS [13]) and three deep learning ones (i.e., FS-Net [6], EBSNN-L and EBSNN-G [5]); and (2) two pre-trained models, i.e., ET-BERT [14] and YaTC [3]. We first evaluate and compare the results of TraCare and the baselines without traffic filtering. Then, in Fig. 11, we further compare different models with and without their corresponding traffic filtering strategies, following the settings in their original papers or open-source implementations.

To further isolate the benefit of TraCare’s activity-aware enhancement module, we select five classical enhancement methods, i.e., Rosetta [29], RIFA [1], SimCSE [41], d-CV [30], and SHF [35]. Among them, d-CV is adopted as an adaptive decision-level filtering baseline, since it adaptively suppresses unreliable single-flow classification through multi-flow result aggregation instead of fixed protocol- or length-based filtering. Rosetta, RIFA, SimCSE, and SHF are adopted as learningbased denoising baselines. Specifically, Rosetta learns traffic semantic invariance through TCP-aware enhancement, RIFA learns to reduce the impact of non-informative header fields through random protocol field augmentation, SimCSE learns consistent traffic representations through contrastive learning, and SHF learns robustness to unstable header fields through header-field shuffling.

Implementation. We implement TraCare and all baselines by using Python 3.10.14 with libraries including NumPy 1.26.14, PyTorch 2.3.0, and CUDA 12.4. As TraCare can be easily transferred to models with different architectures, we implement TraCare by utilizing different pre-trained models during the model pre-training phase. Specifically, these pre-trained models include 1) two existing open-source pre-trained models based on their own corpus, i.e., ET-BERT [14] and YaTC [3]; 2) six pre-trained models based on our proposed traffic corpus, including LSTM-P [65], GRU-P [66], ELMo [67], GPT2 [68], BERT\* [39], and BART [69]. TraCare adopts a sequential optimization strategy for the activity-aware enhancement module and the few-shot fine-tuning module, instead of jointly optimizing them in an end-to-end manner. The activity-aware enhancement module consists of unsupervised clustering and self-supervised contrastive learning. We use Agglomerative Clustering [70] for unsupervised clustering. Since the number of negative samples affects contrastive learning [61], we maximize the batch size within GPU memory limits to increase it. We then apply Grid Search to find the appropriate values for the learning rate and temperature coefficient.

Artifact. The artifacts are open-source and publicly available at https://github.com/lincgcg/TraCare-ICT. Specifically, we provide: (1) the code for the measurement studies on the impact of traffic filtering, including the implementations of different traffic filtering strategies; (2) the data and scripts for constructing the proposed general traffic corpus based on open-source datasets and our self-built dataset (i.e., PrivII); and (3) the training code and scripts for the proposed filteringfree traffic classification framework (i.e., TraCare), including pre-trained model checkpoints for different model architectures, pre-training code, model configurations, and scripts for activity-aware enhancement and model fine-tuning.

Time-disjoint Evaluation. We ensure time-disjoint evaluation to avoid data overlap between pre-training and evaluation. Specifically, we maintain a strict chronological order by using only the data collected prior to the fine-tuning phase for model pre-training. Fig. 10 illustrates the timestamp distribution across all datasets. We use Scapy to extract packet timestamps from the dataset’s pcap file. The timestamp records the creation time of each packet. To ensure time-disjoint evaluation on this task, we use the initial data segments of the USTC-TFC dataset for pre-training and subsequent data segments for fine-tuning. ## B. Overall Classification Results

We evaluate TraCare by assessing its overall performance on four encrypted traffic classification tasks, and examining its impact on each traffic category compared to traffic filtering. Overall Classification Performance. We show the classification performance of TraCare and all baselines on four encrypted traffic classification tasks in TABLE VII. It can be seen that TraCare outperforms all baselines with significant margins across all tasks. For instance, our model achieves the best F1-Score of 0.7785, 0.9577, 0.9995, and 0.9776 on four tasks. These results surpass the second-best results (0.6990, 0.9191, 0.9711, and 0.9444) by 11.37%, 4.20%, 2.93%, and 3.52%, respectively. Besides, we observe that the secondbest results are all achieved by pre-trained models rather than machine learning models. This observation highlights that pretrained models can achieve SoTA results by self-supervised learning on large-scale data. Further, the results show that TraCare outperforms the two pre-trained models, with average improvements of 7.37% over ET-BERT and 6.67% over YaTC across the four tasks. Overall, these results demonstrate that TraCare can outperform all baselines with significant margins. Performance Changes per Traffic Category. We further evaluate the performance changes caused by TraCare in each traffic category. Specifically, we report the percentage of “P”, “S”, and “N” flags as described in §III-D. The difference is that the performance change here is caused by TraCare, not traffic filtering. For this evaluation, we choose TraCare implemented based on two pre-trained models (i.e., ET-BERT and YaTC), to allow a comparative analysis with traffic filtering. We focus on the possible negative impact of TraCare (i.e., performance degradation), thus we combine the results of “P” and “S” flags, labeled as $\mathrm { ^ { 6 6 } P + S ^ { , 5 } }$ in TABLE VIII. As we mentioned in TABLE III, traffic filtering causes significant model performance degradation on 35%, 43%, 47%, and 77% of traffic categories across four tasks, with an average of 50.50%. In contrast, TABLE VIII shows that TraCare only causes model performance degradation on 2%, 5%, 6%, and 8% of traffic categories, with an average of 5.25%. Overall, TraCare reduces the proportion of traffic categories experiencing performance degradation from 50.50% to 5.25%, achieving an average reduction of 89.60% compared to traffic filtering.

Fig. 11 presents the F1-Score results of these models with and without traffic filtering. The results show that traffic filtering has negative effects on those models. For traditional machine learning models (i.e., FP and AS), traffic filtering can support model training by focusing on specific traffic. This is because these models rely on extracting statistical features from specific traffic (e.g., TCP and UDP). However, this reliance on specific traffic features results in poor classification performance. For deep learning models (i.e., FS-Net, EBSNN-L, and EBSNN-G), traffic filtering’s impacts on these models are similar to its impacts on pre-trained models. Specifically, both deep learning and pre-trained models learn diverse traffic behaviors from raw data. When traffic filtering removes noisy data, it inadvertently causes the models to ignore certain traffic behaviors, thereby diminishing their classification performance. As shown in Fig. 11, traffic filtering results in an average F1-Score reduction of 1.19% (ranging from ↑ 0.92% to ↓ 5.73%) for deep learning models.

General Traffic Corpus. We validate the effectiveness of the proposed general traffic corpus by comparing models pre-trained on different corpora. Specifically, we evaluate ET-BERT and BERT\*, where BERT\* adopts the ET-BERT architecture but is pre-trained on our proposed general traffic corpus. Following the setting of the time-disjoint evaluation in §V-A, we focus on the IoT attack identification task to analyze the proposed general traffic corpus, because this task allows us to pre-train BERT\* on the complete traffic corpus, including ISCX-VPN, ISCX-Tor, USTC-TFC, and PrivII datasets. In contrast, for the other tasks, the available pre-training data of the proposed traffic corpus is restricted by the collection time of the downstream datasets. Note that neither ET-BERT nor BERT\* used the dataset from the IoT attack identification task during pre-training. As shown in Fig. 12, BERT\* achieves the F1-Score of 0.9497 on the IoT attack identification task, while ET-BERT achieves 0.8891. This result shows that pretraining on the complete proposed corpus can improve model generalization without using the downstream dataset during pre-training. This improvement indicates the benefit of broader traffic behavior coverage in our corpus. In particular, PrivII increases corpus diversity by introducing traffic from additional network environments and protocols, rather than being designed for any specific downstream task.

Activity-aware Enhancement. We first compare our activityaware enhancement method with five classical enhancement methods [29], [1], [35], [41], [30]. We apply TraCare and these methods to ET-BERT. TABLE IX shows the results using different enhancement methods, where the enhancement rate is the average improvement compared to the no-enhancement results. The results demonstrate that TraCare stably improves performance, achieving an enhancement rate of +3.25%. In contrast, other methods exhibit instability, even causing slight performance degradation. For instance, the enhancement rates for Rosetta and RIFA are -1.02% and -0.18%, respectively. This instability arises because these methods fail to effectively process noisy data in the absence of traffic filtering.

Moreover, we perform activity-aware enhancement on different pre-trained models. As shown in Fig. 13, activityaware enhancement can improve the classification performance across all pre-trained models on different tasks. On average, activity-aware enhancement achieves performance improvements of 6.51%, 3.84%, 4.33%, and 3.03% on four tasks. Notably, activity-aware enhancement is model architectureagnostic, making it adaptable to different pre-trained models. For instance, on the application classification task, it improves the F1-Score by 6.89%, 6.90%, 7.79%, 6.61%, 11.51%, 9.04%, and 8.40% for ET-BERT, YaTC, LSTM-P, GRU-P, ELMo, GPT2, and BART, respectively. These results demonstrate that activity-aware enhancement significantly improves classification performance, regardless of model architecture.

## D. Feasibility Analysis

We further evaluate TraCare’s feasibility under limited resources. To demonstrate model effectiveness when utilizing low-complexity and low-resource models, the following experiments focus on LSTM-P.

Few-shot Learning Scenario. We evaluate TraCare’s effectiveness in label-scarce settings through few-shot experiments, using X labeled samples per traffic category (1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100). Fig. 15 compares the classification performance of TraCare and LSTM-P using different numbers of labeled samples per traffic category on the IoT attack identification task. “LSTM-P + TraCare” represents TraCare implemented based on the LSTM-P model. Notably, in the 10-shot scenario, where the model accesses only 10 labeled data points per traffic category, TraCare significantly outperforms LSTM-P. For instance, TraCare achieves the Precision, Recall, and F1- Score of (0.8526, 0.8339, 0.8616). These results are significantly better than LSTM-P’s performance of (0.7192, 0.7110, 0.6546), even outperforming LSTM-P’s performance in the 100-shot scenario, which is (0.8350, 0.8251, 0.8082). These results demonstrate that TraCare achieves a 90% reduction in labeled data usage compared to the non-enhanced model, while maintaining comparable classification performance.

## VI. DISCUSSION

Overhead of Introducing a Pre-training Task. While pretrained models capture diverse traffic relationships through various pre-training tasks, introducing new tasks greatly increases training overhead. For instance, ET-BERT [14] employs two pre-training tasks: Masked BURST Model (MBM) and Same-origin BURST Prediction (SBP). We compare the training overhead between the original ET-BERT and a version that only employs MBM (ET-BERT-nonSBP). Traversing the entire corpus for pre-training, ET-BERT-nonSBP takes 199.76 minutes, while ET-BERT takes 407.94 minutes, indicating a 104.22% overhead increase. Despite this, introducing pretraining tasks does not always result in performance im-

provements. For example, the F1-Score of ET-BERT-nonSBP and ET-BERT on the application classification and service identification tasks are (0.6955, 0.7086) and (0.9249, 0.9236), respectively. In contrast, TraCare improves classification performance stably with low computational overhead.

Multi-Stage or End-to-End Implementation in Practice. To better adapt to specific tasks, TraCare adopts a multi-stage design that decouples activity-aware enhancement from the pre-training phase. This design also shows practical feasibility in a real-world deployment with a cybersecurity company. The deployment data consists of TLS 1.3 traffic collected from real network environments and covers more than 100 traffic categories. The labels were mainly generated by the company’s mature rule-based labeling system and then corrected by security experts on a small subset. To better reflect real deployment scenarios, we used previously collected traffic for training and newly collected traffic for testing. Compared with ET-BERT, TraCare achieves a macro F1-Score of 0.7921, with a relative improvement of 28.07%. This deployment serves as anecdotal supporting evidence for the practical feasibility of the multistage approach in real-world scenarios. Future work could benefit from jointly optimizing pre-training and activity-aware enhancement. Rather than directly adopting pre-training tasks from CV [3] and NLP [14], it is worth exploring pre-training objectives tailored to the unique characteristics of network traffic in the security domain.

Scalability and Generalizability. As protocols and traffic behaviors evolve over time, traffic classification systems may face scalability and generalizability challenges in long-term deployment. A direct scalability issue is that newly emerging protocols often require manually designing or updating traffic filtering strategies, which incurs substantial human effort. TraCare mitigates this issue through its filtering-free framework, avoiding the need to maintain protocol-specific filtering strategies. In terms of generalizability, real-world deployment may involve continuous changes in traffic behaviors, such as the emergence of new traffic categories and the evolution of existing ones. Although TraCare enhances the model’s understanding of traffic behaviors through self-supervised pretraining on the general traffic corpus, it still cannot effectively classify completely unseen categories without updating the classifier. This issue could be mitigated by class-incremental learning, which we leave as future work.

Time-Split Evaluation. In our measurement study, we follow existing traffic classification studies and adopt a flow-level random split. This enables fair comparison with prior works and avoids introducing additional confounding factors when studying the impact of traffic filtering. Nevertheless, real-world deployment is often chronological, where models trained on historical traffic are applied to future traffic. Therefore, we conduct a time-split evaluation on the malware classification task, on which both existing methods and TraCare achieve superior performance. Specifically, after randomly selecting at most 5,000 flows from each traffic category, we obtain the timestamp of each flow and sort the selected flows chronologically. We then split the ordered flows into training, validation, and testing sets with a ratio of 8:1:1, ensuring that testing flows are temporally later than training flows. We consider two timestamp assignment strategies: using the timestamp of the first packet in each flow and using the timestamp of the last packet in each flow. Compared with the random split, ET-BERT suffers performance drops of 1.12% and 1.32% under the two time-split settings, respectively. TraCare also experiences slight performance drops, but only by 0.15% and 0.11%. These results indicate that time-split evaluation is more challenging than random split, while TraCare remains robust under this more practical temporal setting.

## VII. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we conduct a comprehensive and systematic study to demystify the impact of traffic filtering for encrypted traffic classification. Our detailed analysis reveals that traffic filtering leads to broadly negative impacts for encrypted traffic classification across different phases, categories, and strategies. The primary cause lies in the compromise of traffic fidelity and the inevitable removal of valuable data. Specifically, the same type of data can be either noisy or valuable for different traffic categories; thus, valuable data is inevitably removed during traffic filtering. To this end, we explore the filtering-free framework for encrypted traffic classification and present TraCare to enhance the model’s ability to capture behavioral relevance between noisy data and other traffic. TraCare uses an activityaware enhancement module to capture task-specific behavioral relevance and enhance the model’s understanding of taskspecific noisy data. Moreover, we build a general traffic corpus to enrich the model’s understanding of potentially noisy data. Extensive experiments demonstrate that TraCare can achieve high classification effectiveness and deployment feasibility. We hope this work draws more attention to basic modules (e.g., traffic filtering) in encrypted traffic classification.
