# Practical VPN Fingerprinting using Coarse Inference of Field Specifications in Data Channels

Taewook Kim, Jinhwan Kim, Sangmin Lee, and Yeongpil Cho

Dept. of Computer Science (Automotive-Computer Convergence), Hanyang University, Seoul, Republic of Korea Email: {qkenr7895, adsll156, ozoesm, ypcho}@hanyang.ac.kr

Abstract—VPN fingerprinting techniques are essential for network administrators to mitigate security threats arising from unauthorized or harmful VPN usage. Existing methods often rely on complex, protocol-specific signatures or computationally expensive AI models, limiting their practical applicability. In this paper, we introduce VPNSPOTTER, a practical VPN fingerprinting technique that infers coarse-grained field specifications from consistent data channel packets. VPNSPOTTER employs heuristic filtering to discard control channel packets and inconsistent packets caused by retransmission, segmentation, and aggregation. It then infers coarse-grained field specifications in a column-wise manner, categorizing fields into five predefined types. Evaluations on diverse VPN protocols—including mainstream, proprietary, and obfuscated variants—demonstrate that VPNSPOTTER accurately and efficiently identifies VPN traffic, outperforming prior signature-based and AI-based approaches.

Index Terms—VPN Fingerprinting, Field Specification

## I. INTRODUCTION

Virtual Private Network (VPN) is a technology that provides secure communication over a public network by encapsulating network traffic within a VPN protocol [1]. With VPNs, network packets are encrypted and thus rendered unidentifiable, making VPNs one of the most effective tools for safeguarding privacy from censorship and surveillance. In turn, a variety of VPN tools have been developed and downloaded over 100 million times on the Google Play Store, and some statistics estimate that 31% of internet users worldwide use VPNs [2].

Unfortunately, adopting VPNs can worsen network security by hindering traffic monitoring. Network users leveraging VPNs, for example, may engage in a variety of harmful network activities, such as accessing phishing websites, downloading malware, or using prohibited AI services. To respond to such threats, network administrators need to examine network traffic, for instance by applying Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) techniques. However, this is virtually infeasible due to VPNs’ protection mechanisms that encrypt the network traffic. This fact brings about the importance of a VPN fingerprinting technique, which detects the presence of VPN-protected flows on the network. With this technique, administrators can implement a whitelisting strategy in VPN management, specifically by blocking unauthorized or suspicious VPN flows, thereby mitigating VPN-related network threats.

VPN fingerprinting is challenging because (1) VPN traffic is inherently unidentifiable due to encryption, (2) diverse VPN protocols have been deployed, and (3) VPNs sometimes obfuscate traffic to hamper detailed analysis. VPN fingerprinting techniques can be categorized into two approaches: signaturebased and AI-based. The signature-based approach [3]–[7] is based on the fact that most VPN protocols exhibit at least a few distinct features in their handshaking procedures and field specifications. Such per-VPN features can serve as unique signatures, enabling VPN fingerprinting. However, this approach comes with the drawback of requiring significant effort to handle various VPN protocols, each of which has complex field specifications or even employs obfuscation. The AI approach [8]–[16] mitigates these limitations by leveraging a machine learning (ML) model trained to identify VPN protocols based on features automatically inferred from large volumes of packets. This approach provides high extensibility and notable accuracy without in-depth understanding of each protocol and thus has been widely adopted in VPN fingerprinting studies. Unfortunately, this approach usually incurs performance overheads while constantly running computationally expensive ML models, which can become considerable as the amount of network traffic increases.

In this paper, we propose VPNSPOTTER, a practical VPN fingerprinting technique that employs the signature-based approach to avoid the overhead associated with the AI-based approach. Note that the signature-based approach requires determining fingerprinting signatures for each VPN protocol. Previous works [3] addressed this by extracting signatures from the control channel, which is used for session establishment. However, this method involved significant effort due to varying field specifications and complex packet flows in the control channel. In contrast, VPNSPOTTER focuses on the data channel, which is used for data transmission. This shift is effective because the data channel in VPN protocols tends to have consistent field specifications, allowing VPNSPOT-TER to use them as signatures for VPN fingerprinting, as described in Section II. Notably, VPNSPOTTER infers these specifications at a coarse granularity, enabling more efficient processing. Even at this coarse level, the inferred specifications are sufficiently distinctive to differentiate VPN protocols from one another and from non-VPN traffic, as demonstrated in our evaluation.

Once a VPN flow is delivered, VPNSPOTTER first identifies the data channel, which follows the control channel. Since control channel packets can be retransmitted, VPNSPOTTER employs a heuristic method to filter out such packets and roughly determine the transition point. After the data channel is identified, VPNSPOTTER infers coarse-grained field specifications in a column-wise manner from aligned data channel packets. However, in TCP-based VPN protocols, packet segmentation and aggregation [17] pose challenges, as they disrupt alignment and hinder column-wise inference of field specifications. To remedy this, VPNSPOTTER applies heuristic filtering methods leveraging three key indicators: zero-filled values, inferred length values, and the latency gap. By integrating these filtering methods, VPNSPOTTER effectively mitigates both segmentation and aggregation issues, improving the reliability of the coarsely inferred field specifications.

By taking advantage of the coarse-grained field specification in the data channel, VPNSPOTTER provides a practical VPN fingerprinting that excels in terms of both efficiency and accuracy. To evaluate VPNSPOTTER, we test it using carefully collected network traffic that contains VPN and non-VPN flows. Experimental results show that VPNSPOTTER achieves superior identification accuracy compared to both existing signature-based and AI-based solutions, successfully distinguishing not only mainstream VPN protocols but also proprietary and some obfuscated VPN protocols from non-VPN protocols with minimal false positives/negatives. Moreover, it performs 1.7x and 8.8x faster than GPU-accelerated and CPU-only AI-based approaches, respectively.

The contributions of this research are as follows:

• We propose VPNSPOTTER, a practical VPN fingerprinting technique that focuses on the consistently structured data channel rather than the control channel.

• VPNSPOTTER uses lightweight heuristic methods to infer coarse-grained field specifications without relying on AI or protocol-specific expert knowledge.

• Through extensive evaluation with various VPN and non-VPN protocols, we demonstrate that VPNSPOTTER can identify VPN protocols more efficiently and accurately than previous AI-based and signature-based solutions.

## II. MOTIVATION

In this section, we highlight common characteristics of VPN protocols and explain how they shape our approach.

## A. Common Characteristics of VPN

VPN protocols typically consist of an initial control channel followed by a data channel. The control channel is responsible for a variety of session initialization operations, such as negotiating configurations and exchanging encryption keys, whereas the data channel is dedicated to a single operation of transmitting encrypted user traffic.

The field specification—which defines what fields are included in packets and how their sizes and positions are arranged—depends largely on the operations being performed. Because the control channel handles diverse and complex operations, its field specification tends to vary within a session according to the context. In contrast, the data channel, serving a uniform operation, typically exhibits simpler and more consistent field specifications throughout the session.

To be specific, data channel packets generally consist of a VPN header and a VPN payload. The payload carries the encrypted user traffic, while the header contains several fields to support more secure and efficient transmission of the payload. For security, a MAC field ensures message integrity and a counter field prevents packet replay attacks. For performance, message type and session identifier fields indicate the purpose of packets and sessions they belong to, respectively, facilitating efficient VPN communications by clarifying how each packet should be handled.

## B. Our Approach

Based on common characteristics observed across VPN protocols, our approach is to leverage the consistent field specifications observed in the data channel for VPN fingerprinting. To achieve this, we first identify the data channel within the network traffic, and then coarsely infer its consistent field specification. The inferred specification is used as a feature to determine whether the traffic belongs to a VPN protocol. This approach is feasible because, as described below, VPN and non-VPN protocols generally exhibit distinguishable field specifications in their data channels.

## III. THREAT MODEL

We assume a scenario where VPNs compromise network security. For example, network users might exploit VPNs, which encrypt network traffic, to circumvent security monitoring while engaging in harmful network activities. These activities could include using AI services prohibited on the network or leaking internal data. In response to this threat, network administrators can utilize VPNSPOTTER, a VPN fingerprinting technique. They can run VPNSPOTTER to monitor network traffic to determine whether a VPN is in use and, if so, identify which VPN protocol is being employed. Based on these fingerprinting results, they can take follow-up measures, such as disconnecting unauthorized VPN connections or determining specific applications encapsulated within the VPN tunnels [16].

## IV. DESIGN

We propose a VPN fingerprinting technique, VPNSPOT-TER, which identifies VPN protocols based on field specifications coarsely inferred from the data channel. This section first presents an overview of VPNSPOTTER, then describes each step of the technique in detail.

## A. VPNSPOTTER Overview

Figure 2 illustrates the overview of VPNSPOTTER. For a given network flow, VPNSPOTTER attempts to perform VPN fingerprinting through three stages. In the first stage (Section IV-B), VPNSPOTTER filters out inconsistent packets in the flow, including control channel and segmented/aggregated data channel packets, in order to minimize noise in the subsequent analysis. In the second stage (Section IV-C), from the filtered consistent data channel packets, VPNSPOTTER infers coarse-grained field specifications using a columnwise method. Simply put, it categorizes each field into predefined types—length, stable, increment, high-entropy, and low-entropy—that are commonly found in VPN protocols, by statistically analyzing value variations at the same column position across packets. Lastly, in the third stage (Section IV-D), VPNSPOTTER compares the inferred field specifications against a database of known VPN specifications to determine whether the network flow is truly running over a VPN protocol.

## B. Inconsistent Packet Filtering

Uniquely, VPNSPOTTER performs VPN fingerprinting through the data channel, which exhibits consistent field specifications in VPN protocols. To this end, in the first stage, VPNSPOTTER filters out two types of inconsistent packets: (1) control channel packets and (2) segmented or aggregated TCP data channel packets. The following subsections explain each type of inconsistent packets and how to filter them out.

1) Control Channel Packet Filtering: To focus on the data channel, packets belonging to the control channel must be filtered out. A na¨ıve approach to do so is to identify them based on deep knowledge of VPN protocols, but this can reduce practicality. Thus, VPNSPOTTER employs the following heuristic methods instead.

Initial Control Channel Packets. As described in Section II-A, the control channel precedes the data channel in a session. With this in mind, VPNSPOTTER filters out the control channel by discarding the initial $n _ { c u t o f f }$ packets which are expected to belong to the control channel. VPNSPOTTER determines $n _ { c u t o f f }$ empirically based on analysis of known VPN protocols, as described in Section V-B1. Certainly, this heuristic method cannot precisely filter out the control channel when $n _ { c u t o f f }$ is overestimated or underestimated. Fortunately, minor errors in $n _ { c u t o f f }$ do not harm the subsequent field specification in the data channel because our column-wise inference method is based on statistical aggregation as described in Section IV-C

Retransmitted Control Channel Packets. In the control channel, VPN protocols verify whether the peer has correctly received the session initialization information. If the server does not respond due to network congestion occurring in the control channel, the VPN client retransmits control packets. This behavior can significantly increase the number of control channel packets, leading to a considerable underestimation of $n _ { c u t o f f }$ . In our experiments, under severe congestion with 90% packet loss rate, the number of control channel packets increased more than fivefold compared to a non-congestion case with 0% packet loss rate. Moreover, it’s common for users to connect to geographically distant VPN servers—for example, connecting to a VPN server located in the U.S. in order to use Google Maps in China—which makes network congestion more likely.

With TCP-based VPNs, retransmissions happen at the transport layer, so filtering out retransmitted packets is straightforward using sequence numbers in the TCP header. However, as UDP-based VPNs handle retransmissions at the application layer, such explicit identifiers do not exist. Fortunately, most UDP-based VPN protocols exhibit distinguishable transmission timing patterns, which VPNSPOTTER can exploit to identify retransmissions of UDP packets. For instance, in the control channel, WireGuard retransmits initiation requests every five seconds if the server does not respond, whereas OpenVPN and StrongSwan [18] (an IPsec-based VPN implementation) retransmit using initial timeouts of 2 and 4 seconds, respectively. Based on these observations, VPNSPOTTER determines that a retransmission has occurred if two consecutive packets are sent from the client to the server and the time interval between them exceeds a certain threshold (1 second in our experiments). Once a retransmission is detected in this manner, VPNSPOTTER treats that point as the new start of the control channel and cuts off the next $n _ { c u t o f f }$ packets, just as it does for the initial control channel packets.

2) Segmented/Aggregated TCP Data Channel Packet Filtering: VPNSPOTTER filters out not only control channel packets but also certain data channel packets that lose consistency due to TCP segmentation or aggregation. Unlike UDP, TCP is a stream-oriented protocol that can split a single application-layer message across multiple packets, as well as aggregate multiple small writes into a single packet [17]. These segmentation and aggregation behaviors are also observed in TCP-based VPNs. Accordingly, data channel packets can be categorized into normal packets, which are neither segmented nor aggregated, header-begin packets, which begin with a VPN header but are segmented or aggregated, and payloadbegin packets, which begin directly with a VPN payload. These payload-begin packets negatively impact the accuracy of field specifications in the data channel, as their encrypted contents introduce significant noise into the column-wise inference stage. Consequently, VPNSPOTTER needs to filter out these payload-begin packets from the data channel before proceeding to the next stage.

To filter out these packets, VPNSPOTTER applies the following three heuristic rules: (1) zero-based filtering, (2) lengthbased filtering, and (3) latency-based filtering. VPNSPOTTER determines that a packet is a non-payload-begin packet—either header-begin or normal—if it satisfies two or more rules.

Zero-based Filtering. As VPN payload is encrypted, payloadbegin packets are filled with values that have no discernible patterns. In contrast, non-payload-begin packets have some recognizable header fields (e.g., counter), whose most significant bits are often zeros. Thus, we conclude that any packet containing a certain number (16 by default) of consecutive zero bits—uncommon in VPN payload—is likely to be a normal or a header-begin packet.

Length-based Filtering. TCP-based VPN protocols sometimes embed length information in the VPN header to facilitate reassembly. In such cases, normal packets have the same length values in both the VPN header and the IP header. In contrast, header-begin and payload-begin packets surely show different length values between the two headers. Therefore, if the length value specified in the IP header also appears within the TCP payload—which presumably contains a VPN header—the packet is considered a normal one.

Latency-based Filtering. We measured time intervals between packets in the data channel and observed that payloadbegin packets tend to have short inter-packet intervals compared to other types of packets. This is because the segmentation is conducted at the lower layer, TCP, allowing for efficient and streamlined delivery than if performed at the application layer. Based on this observation, we classify a packet as a payload-begin if its inter-arrival time falls within a certain lower percentile (40% by default) of all measured intervals.

## C. Field Specification Inference

After filtering, VPNSPOTTER proceeds to the second stage, where it infers field specifications in a column-wise manner from consistent packets that are either header-begin or normal ones. Note that protocol reverse engineering is not the primary goal of VPNSPOTTER. In this context, VPNSPOTTER deliberately performs a coarse-grained inference, which is efficient and still sufficiently distinctive for VPN fingerprinting. In a word, VPNSPOTTER infers only five predefined types of fields from the packets. This means that the original protocol cannot be fully reconstructed. However, since these field types are commonly found across many VPN protocols and their layouts differ between protocols, VPNSPOTTER can leverage them as effective features for VPN fingerprinting.

1) Inference Window: To reduce inference time, VP-NSPOTTER analyzes only a limited number of packets—typically a few dozen—from each flow. It is well known that statistical values such as the mean or variance converge to their true values at a rate of $O ( 1 / \sqrt { n } )$ as the sample size n increases. This means that when VPNSPOTTER uses about 50 samples, the standard error becomes ±14%. This level of error seems considerable but acceptable in VPNSPOTTER because its coarse-grained inference focuses only on specific field types with large differences in variance. For example, fixed-value fields have variances close to zero, whereas counter or length fields exhibit intermediate variances roughly between 10 and 100, and high-entropy fields such as encrypted payload have variances in the thousands or more. As such large variance gaps are observed among field types, VPNSPOTTER is able to reliably distinguish field types using only a few dozen packets, which aligns with previous protocol reverse engineering studies [19].

For selected packets, VPNSPOTTER further narrows its analysis to only a few tens of initial bytes, rather than processing the entire packet. This design choice to improve efficiency is viable because VPN packets are predominantly composed of encrypted payloads, whereas distinguishable and inferable field types are mostly confined to headers near the start of the packet.

2) Inference Field Types: VPNSPOTTER coarsely categorizes fields into five predefined types according to their value-change patterns: length, stable, increment, high-entropy, and low-entropy. Once a session starts, VPNSPOTTER takes tens of packets and attempts to infer fields along with their types in a column-wise manner. Specifically, VPNSPOTTER statistically analyzes the values in the same column across those packets and classifies them into one of the predefined types. If the values are identified as different types, they are inferred to be of the majority type. Each field type can be identified as follows.

Length Field. Column values that are equal to the total-length field in the IP header (as in normal packets), or partially different from it (as in header-begin packets), are identified as length fields. In some VPN protocols, the high-order bits of this field are repurposed as bit flags. To handle this, VPNSPOTTER matches only the lower bits corresponding to the significant bits of the total-length field, rather than the entire field.

Stable Field. Columns with fixed values across packets are identified as stable fields. Examples include message type and session identifier fields.

Increment Field. Columns whose values increase monotonically across packets are identified as increment fields. A typical example is a counter field.

High-Entropy Field. Columns with values exhibiting high entropy are identified as high-entropy fields. Encrypted payloads and MAC fields in VPN protocols are included in this type of field. To identify this field type, VPNSPOTTER employs the Great Firewall of China’s bit-density test [5], which counts the number of set bits per byte. According to this test, if the average set-bit count lies between 3.4 and 4.6 bits per byte, the values are considered high-entropy type.

Low-Entropy Field. Columns that do not satisfy the criteria for any of the above types are classified as low-entropy fields. Examples include textual fields that change intermittently.

## D. VPN Protocol Identification

After completing field inference, VPNSPOTTER proceeds to the last stage of identifying the VPN protocol based on the inferred field specification. Specifically, VPNSPOTTER compares the inferred field specifications against a database of pre-extracted field specifications from known VPN protocols. If a match with a known VPN protocol is found, the flow is classified as VPN. Here, constructing the field specification database is straightforward. For instance, a network administrator who aims to identify a specific VPN protocol, such as OpenVPN, can collect the network traffic of that VPN protocol and then run VPNSPOTTER to infer its own field specification, which can be added into the database. Since the inference of field specifications is fully automated, VPNSPOTTER eliminates the need for protocol-specific knowledge, unlike previous signature-based solutions [3].

## V. EVALUATION

To evaluate VPNSPOTTER, we collected network traffic that included both VPN and non-VPN packets and applied VPNSPOTTER to pick out consistent data packets, infer the field specifications, and then identify VPN protocols in turn. By doing so, we answered the following questions:

• RQ 1: How effectively can VPNSPOTTER filter inconsistent packets? (Section V-B)

• RQ 2: Can VPN protocols be identified using the field specifications inferred by VPNSPOTTER? (Section V-C)

• RQ 3: How accurately and efficiently can VPNSPOTTER identify VPN protocols? (Section V-D)

## A. Dataset

1) VPN Dataset: Since most VPN fingerprinting studies have not publicly shared their datasets due to ethical considerations, we collected our own VPN dataset. Although the ISCXVPN2016 dataset [20] has been used in some related works, our inspection and an independent analysis [15] failed to identify the tell-tale signatures of known VPN protocols from this dataset. USBVPN [21] is another public VPN dataset, but it provides only limited metadata of packets except for payloads. We therefore excluded both ISCXVPN2016 and USBVPN from our evaluation.

We selected 19 commercial VPN services based on recommendations from popular VPN review sites [2], citations in prior research [3], and high review counts (over 10,000) on the Chrome Web Store. We excluded services whose servers were unreachable or lacked Windows clients. For each selected service we captured VPN traffic using mainstream VPN protocols such as OpenVPN, WireGuard, and IPsec-based VPNs, as well as proprietary or obfuscated variants—including Lightway, OpenVPN XOR, WireGuard over TCP, etc.—often used to evade censorship. To maximize diversity, we varied server location settings (e.g., Asia, Europe, and America) and client activities (e.g., YouTube streaming, TikTok browsing, and Wikipedia reading). In total, we collected 1,899 VPN flows.

2) Non-VPN Dataset: We also collected non-VPN dataset to evaluate whether VPNSPOTTER incorrectly classifies non-VPN traffic as VPN protocol. The dataset includes the non-VPN portion of ISCXVPN2016, which offers labeled traces of common network activities such as web browsing, e-mail, and media streaming. The dataset also contains publicly available sample traces provided by Wireshark [22], which cover a wide range of protocols, including TLS, SSH, and IoT-oriented ZigBee. Lastly, the dataset incorporates background traffic captured on our university network over two weeks. In total, we collected 1,907,297 non-VPN network flows.

2) TCP-Segmented/Aggregated Data Channel Packet Filtering: We evaluated the effectiveness of the filtering method described in Section IV-B2 for TCP-segmented/aggregated data channel packets. In the evaluation, we adjusted three filtering thresholds: (1) the maximum run-length of consecutive zero bits for zero-based filtering $( n _ { z e r o } ) _ { \cdot }$ , (2) the lower percentile cutoff for inter-packet intervals in latency-based filtering $( p _ { l a t e n c y } )$ , and (3) the minimum number of heuristic rules (out of three) that a packet should satisfy to avoid being classified as a payload-begin packet $( n _ { r u l e } )$ . While strict thresholds can effectively remove payload-begin packets, they may also eliminate a significant number of non-payload-begin packets, that is header-begin or normal, resulting in too few packets remaining for analysis. Since censorship is generally preferred to be performed with as few sample packets as possible [3], [23], we set the thresholds by balancing these two factors. We define the filtering outcomes as follows:

Precision Indicates the fraction of packets predicted as nonpayload-begin that are actually non-payload-begin. Higher precision means VPNSPOTTER more effectively removes noise caused by TCP segmentation/aggregation.

Recall Indicates the fraction of actual non-payload-begin packets that are correctly identified. Higher recall means VPNSPOTTER can perform subsequent field specification inference with more non-payload-begin packets.

F1 Score The harmonic mean of precision and recall. It helps identify the optimal combination of the three filtering thresholds—maximizing the removal of payload-begin packets while retaining as many non-payload-begin packets as possible.

## C. Inferred Field Specification of VPN Protocols

We investigated whether the coarse-grained field specifications inferred by VPNSPOTTER can serve as reliable fingerprints for VPN protocol identification. Figure 6 presents the field specifications inferred by VPNSPOTTER for VPN protocols, well-known cryptographic protocols, and five randomly selected network flows from our non-VPN dataset.

1) Distinguishable VPN Protocols: We were able to identify mainstream, proprietary, and some obfuscated VPN protocols, as they have field specifications distinguishable from cryptographic and non-VPN protocols.

OpenVPN, WireGuard, and IPsec-based VPNs. These protocols [18], [24], [25] are widely deployed across VPN services and have field specifications that can be clearly distinguishable from those of non-VPN protocols. Although OpenVPN UDP and IPsec-based VPNs share the same inferred specification, it still differs from that of non-VPN protocols, so our goal of separating VPN from non-VPN traffic remains unaffected. Furthermore, both protocols provide welldocumented formats, making it easy to obtain fingerprints that can differentiate OpenVPN UDP from IPsec-based VPNs.

Lightway UDP. ExpressVPN, one of the most popular VPN services, has developed its own protocol called Lightway [26], designed to provide faster and more reliable connections. Lightway supports both TCP and UDP modes, encapsulating traffic with TLS and dTLS, respectively. Unlike the TCP version, the UDP version prepends 16-byte extra protocol fields (e.g., version, reserved, session identifier) ahead of the dTLS header. Thanks to these additional fields, Lightway remains distinguishable from plain dTLS even though the protocol is built on top of it.

Obfuscated OpenVPN. Known as Scrambled OpenVPN or XOR OpenVPN, this protocol obfuscates ordinary OpenVPN traffic [27]. In the UDP version, it XOR-encrypts every byte of each message and shuffles the bytes, but—as previous work has shown [3]—the 1st byte of every message, the opcode field, is never shuffled and is always masked with the same key. Because the opcode remains fixed and deterministically encoded, it is still classified as a stable type after obfuscation, while the rest of the packet becomes high-entropy. That single stable byte is enough to distinguish obfuscated OpenVPN from non-VPN protocols like QUIC or SSH, whose field specifications are all composed of high-entropy types. In the TCP variant, a 2-byte length header is added after obfuscation, and the following opcode byte remains stable, as in UDP. As a result, its field specification is clearly distinct from those of any non-VPN protocols.

WireGuard over TCP. Some commercial VPN services, such as Mullvad VPN, allow UDP-based WireGuard VPN to run over TCP in network environments where UDP traffic is restricted. Alternatively, tunneling tools like udptunnel [28] can also facilitate WireGuard over TCP. We collected traffic traces from both cases and analyzed them using VPNSPOTTER. The result revealed that they share the same distinctive field specification where an extra 2-byte of a length field type is prepended to the regular field specification of WireGuard, making them distinguishable from non-VPN protocols.

Proprietary OpenVPN and WireGuard. As the previous study [3] noted, some VPN services offer proprietary versions of OpenVPN and WireGuard, whose format differ from the standard ones. For example, AstrillVPN’s OpenVPN packets apply different layouts depending on direction (client to server or server to client), and in AstrillVPN’s WireGuard the 9th—corresponding to the increment field in standard WireGuard—increases or decreases by 1, so VPNSPOTTER treats it as a low-entropy. Although we have no knowledge of these proprietary protocols, the field specification inferred by VPNSPOTTER provides unique fingerprints.

OpenConnect. The VPN service VeePN provides a protocol called OpenConnect. This protocol exhibits a unique field specification that allows VPNSPOTTER to successfully distinguish it from non-VPN traffic.

2) Indistinguishable VPN Protocols: Some obfuscated VPN protocols had field specifications identical to those of cryptographic protocols, making them indistinguishable by VPNSPOTTER.

TLS Encapsulated Protocols. To evade censorship, some VPN protocols cloak their traffic within SSL/TLS [7]. Examples include SSTP [29], Hydra Catapult [30], NordWhisper [31], and Lightway TCP, all of which run over a TLS tunnel. In such cases, VPNSPOTTER is unable to distinguish VPN traffic from ordinary (non-VPN) TLS traffic, as both share the same field specification.

## D. VPN Protocol Identification Performance

We evaluated how accurately and efficiently VPNSPOT-TER identifies VPN protocols. Specifically, we assessed its classification accuracy using VPN TPR (VPN True Positive Rate, how many VPN flows are correctly classified as VPN) and Non-VPN FPR (Non-VPN False Positive Rate, how many non-VPN flows are incorrectly classified as VPN), and measured its efficiency in terms of throughput (the number of flows processed per second). Based on these metrics, we analyzed the performance of VPNSPOTTER under different inference window sizes. We then compared its best-performing configuration—including a hybrid approach (VPNSPOTTER+CNN-based classifier) that re-classifies flows flagged as indistinguishable by VPNSPOTTER using a CNN-based classifier—against existing solutions from both signature-based and AI-based approaches—specifically, the Opcode-based and ACK-based fingerprinting techniques [3] that are specialized for OpenVPN, and a CNN-based classifier [12]—all of which, to the best of our knowledge, achieve state-of-the-art performance. Since neither of these previous solutions provided their code publicly, we reimplemented their solutions according to the descriptions provided in their papers. All experiments were performed on an Intel Xeon Gold 6142 machine equipped with 128 GB of RAM and an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (used only by the AI-based approaches).

Table I(i and ii) show the performance of VPNSPOTTER as the inference window varies. When either the number of analyzed packets $( n _ { p a c k e t } )$ or the number of initial packet bytes inspected $( n _ { b y t e } )$ decreases, throughput increases but identification accuracy drops. Specifically, when $n _ { p a c k e t }$ is small, the accuracy falls because the insufficient number of samples increases the possibility that high-entropy fields are mistakenly inferred as low-entropy. Likewise, small $\boldsymbol { n _ { b y t e } }$ degrades the accuracy by making certain VPN and non-VPN protocols indistinguishable. For example, as shown in Figure 6, in Lightway UDP, the first 17 bytes consist entirely of stable fields; therefore if $\scriptstyle n _ { b y t e }$ is less than 17, it may be misidentified as a non-VPN protocol like facebook\_audio. Overall, setting $n _ { p a c k e t } = 5 0$ and $n _ { b y t e } = 2 4$ yields the best balance between accuracy and throughput.

Table I(iii) illustrates the performance comparison of VP-NSPOTTER (and the hybrid approach) against prior solutions. VPNSPOTTER correctly classified all the distinguishable VPN protocols (OV, WG, IPSEC, LW, OC) listed in Section V-C1. On the other hand, it failed to separate the two indistinguishable VPN protocols (TE, FO) listed in Section V-C2 from cryptographic protocols due to their identical field specifications. However, this limitation can be alleviated through the hybrid approach, with a slight trade-off in throughput and the non-VPN FPR. Compared to VPNSPOTTER, the Opcodebased and ACK-based fingerprinting techniques correctly identified only 63% and 19% of OpenVPN traffic. The Opcodebased technique assumes that OpenVPN uses fewer than ten distinct message types. However, the assumption was broken due to segmented or aggregated TCP packets, resulting in decreased accuracy in detecting TCP-based OpenVPN flows. The ACK-based technique utilizes the distribution of OpenVPN ACK messages and assumes the third message in OpenVPN sessions is always an ACK. However, we observed numerous OpenVPN flows where the third message was not an ACK, causing misclassification. Moreover, the non-VPN QUIC protocol stores its message type field at the same position as OpenVPN, and the characteristics of QUIC message types closely resemble those of OpenVPN, leading both Opcodebased and ACK-based fingerprinting techniques to falsely label QUIC as VPN. Since VPNSPOTTER inspects all payload data within the inference window, it was 13x and 21x slower than the two signature-based solutions that only observe the message type field. At least, however, the flow volume that VPNSPOTTER handled was comparable to the prior work [12] that aims at real-world VPN censorship scenarios. In addition, unlike these existing solutions that are protocol-specific (in their cases, OpenVPN), VPNSPOTTER can support a diverse range of VPN protocols.

Turning to the CNN-based classifier, it demonstrated the ability to identify even highly obfuscated VPN protocols (TE, FO) that were indistinguishable by VPNSPOTTER. We attribute this to the AI-based approach’s capability to extract subtle features, such as packet length/timing patterns, that are unseen in the field specifications. For the other VPN protocols (OV, WG, IPSEC, LW, OC), however, it could identify 95% of VPN protocols, which was somewhat less than VPNSPOTTER that showed complete identification. Regarding throughput, the CPU-only VPNSPOTTER could process 1.7x more flows than the GPU-accelerated CNN-based classifier (and 8.8x more than the CPU-only CNN-based classifier), offering substantial cost savings and making it a more practical solution.

## VI. LIMITATION

As stated in Section V-C, VPNSPOTTER exhibits the limitation in distinguishing TLS-encapsulated or fully obfuscated VPN protocols from cryptographic protocols. Fortunately, it can be alleviated as follows. First, in security-sensitive environments such as corporate networks, network administrators who implement censorship often deploy TLS visibility appliances, which act as proxies to capture decrypted TLS sessions [36], [37]. While primarily intended for TLS traffic inspection, such appliances can enable VPNSPOTTER to work on TLS-encapsulated VPNs. Second, effective yet costly AIbased approaches can be selectively applied to indistinguishable protocols, improving identification accuracy while minimizing computational overhead, as shown in Section V-D.

## VII. RELATED WORK

In this section we relate VPNSPOTTER to two areas of research: VPNfingerprinting, at which VPNSPOTTER ultimately aims, and protocol reverse engineering, to which its columnwise field specification method belongs.

## A. VPN Fingerprinting

VPN fingerprinting is a research topic that aims to determine whether network behaviors are VPN-related. We classify relevant studies according to their underlying techniques.

Signature-based Approach. This approach performs VPN fingerprinting using signatures that are distinctive enough to differentiate VPN protocols. A prior work [3] designed a heuristic algorithm based on the characteristics observed in OpenVPN’s control channel messages to identify OpenVPN traffic. Another line of works [4], [5] analyzed the fingerprinting techniques used by the Great Firewall of China, which identify obfuscated protocols (e.g., obfs4) by examining the randomness of initial session packets. Other works [6], [7] proposed algorithms that exploit unique behaviors of tunneling VPN protocols, such as TCP handshake patterns appearing in UDP traffic or TLS characteristics in non-TLS traffic—both of which do not occur in normal, non-encapsulated traffic. However, unlike VPNSPOTTER, which can automatically identify a wide range of VPN protocols, these signature-based approaches are protocol-specific and therefore less scalable.

Machine Learning-based Approach. This approach takes advantage of ML models trained to capture VPN-specific features that are often imperceptible to humans, thereby enabling the identification of VPN protocols. Specifically, relevant works classified VPN traffic using traditional ML models such as random forests [8] or deep learning models such as CNNs [9]– [12], based on session-level statistics [13], packet length or packet inter-arrival time sequences [14], [15], and raw packet content [16] as input features. Although these methods can identify VPN protocols without requiring expert protocol knowledge similar to VPNSPOTTER, due to the inherent computational complexity of machine learning models, they incur higher costs compared to signature-based approaches such as VPNSPOTTER.

Active Probing. This technique verifies whether a server runs a VPN protocol by sending crafted messages and checking if the responses match protocol-specific behavior. One study [3] designed OpenVPN-specific probing messages and identified OpenVPN servers. Other studies [38], [39] leveraged differences in response times or TCP behavior between VPN and non-VPN servers. A recent study [40] identified VPN servers based on unique communication patterns and behaviors that are only exhibited by VPNs. Notably, such active probing can serve as a valuable complement to VPN fingerprinting techniques, including VPNSPOTTER, by providing additional confidence in identification results.

## B. Protocol Reverse Engineering

Protocol reverse engineering is a technique used to uncover detailed specifications of network protocols, including field layouts, field semantics, and stateful message flows, from captured raw traffic. Some studies [41] identified protocol keyword fields by performing multi-sequence alignment across similar messages. Another study [42] used statistical and entropy-based analysis to infer field types such as lengths, constants, and counters. A more recent work [43] combined multiple detectors to jointly infer field boundaries, data types, and serialization patterns. Compared to these studies, VP-NSPOTTER employs a column-wise field inference method that yields a comparatively coarse specification. However, as demonstrated in the evaluation, this level of granularity is sufficient to effectively distinguish VPN protocols from others. Furthermore, by focusing on data channels in which packet structures tend to be consistent, VPNSPOTTER can reliably infer field specifications for VPN fingerprinting.

## VIII. CONCLUSION

We introduced VPNSPOTTER, a practical VPN fingerprinting technique. The core of VPNSPOTTER is the coarse inference of field specifications from the data channel, which serve as sufficient signatures to identify various VPN protocols. To implement this, VPNSPOTTER employs lightweight heuristic algorithms that filter out inconsistent packets—such as control channel packets and TCP-segmented or aggregated packets—enabling more reliable field specification inference. We evaluated VPNSPOTTER on a wide range of VPN protocols and confirmed that it outperforms existing signature-based and AI-based techniques in terms of identification accuracy and efficiency, respectively.
