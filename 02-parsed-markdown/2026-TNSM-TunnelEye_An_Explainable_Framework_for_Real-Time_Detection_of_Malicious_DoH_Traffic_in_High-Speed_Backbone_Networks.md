# TunnelEye: An Explainable Framework for Real-Time Detection of Malicious DoH Traffic in High-Speed Backbone Networks

Jiajia Du , Songtao Liu , Cheng Zhi , Junfan Zhao , Hua Wu , Member, IEEE, and Guang Cheng , Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract—Since plaintext DNS queries are vulnerable to eavesdropping and tampering, encrypted DNS protocols such as DoH (DNS over HTTPS) have been widely adopted. However, attackers can exploit DoH by hiding malicious DNS requests and responses in HTTPS traffic, enabling covert communication and evading detection. Existing methods rely on generic bidirectional traffic statistics that lack interpretability, are costly to compute, and may become unavailable or unstable in backbone monitoring nodes with massive unidirectional and sampled traffic. To address these issues, we propose TunnelEye, an explainable real-time framework for malicious DoH detection. It uses protocol-level behavioral analysis to capture DoH features that remain stable across traffic directions and sampling rates, and introduces an efficient data structure, DoH-Sketch, to ensure real-time performance. Experiments on public and self-collected datasets show that TunnelEye enables second-level detection with high accuracy. On public datasets, it achieves 99.88% accuracy. In backbone networks with sampled traffic, TunnelEye outperforms existing methods, improving accuracy by 14.5% at a 1/128 sampling rate and reducing the false positive rate by 31% under interference from background traffic.

Index Terms—DNS over HTTPS, malicious DoH traffic detection, explainability, backbone network, packet sampling.

## I. INTRODUCTION

However, the DNS protocol transmits data in plaintext, which has long posed security risks [1]. Attackers can easily intercept, alter, or forge DNS queries to steal sensitive information or attack maliciously [2], [3], [4]. Moreover, due to DNS traffic’s critical role in network operations, security mechanisms such as firewalls typically allow DNS traffic to pass through unimpeded, creating an opportunity for malicious DNS traffic (such as covert data transfers via DNS tunneling) to bypass conventional security defenses. 90% of companies had encountered DNS attacks, resulting in significant financial losses [5]. Roughly one-third of DNS attacks were associated with DNS tunneling [6].

To enhance the security of the DNS protocol, the IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force) introduced a series of encrypted DNS protocols [7], [8], [9], among which DNS over HTTPS (DoH) [8] proposed in 2018 encrypts DNS traffic via the HTTPS protocol. With the broad support of HTTPS across existing web infrastructure, DoH quickly became the most widely deployed encrypted DNS protocol. Globally, more than 40 DNS service providers support DoH, including major providers like Cloudflare, Google, and Quad9. On the client side, popular browsers like Firefox [10] and Chrome [11] offer official secure DNS services by configuring DoH. By encrypting DNS requests and responses, DoH effectively prevents man-in-the-middle attacks, traffic eavesdropping, and DNS poisoning, thereby enhancing the privacy and security of DNS communications.

However, the widespread adoption of DoH also introduces new security challenges. The encryption of DoH traffic prevents direct DNS inspection. Attackers can abuse DoH as a covert tunnel, known as a DoH tunneling attack. In such attacks, attackers create and control DoH tunnels. They encapsulate non-DNS payloads (like C2 commands or exfiltrated data) within crafted DNS queries and responses. This enables malicious goals such as command-and-control (C2) and data exfiltration. In this paper, we focus on detecting malicious DoH traffic generated by DoH tunneling attacks. Due to the encryption of DoH, traditional malicious DNS tunneling detection methods [12], [13] that rely on plaintext information become ineffective.

Existing research [14], [15], [16] has proposed several methods for detecting malicious DoH traffic. Most of these methods require grouping packets into flows and extracting complex statistical features, which results in inefficient detection in high-speed backbone networks. Although these methods have been validated in Local Area Networks (LANs), backbone networks handle significantly larger traffic volumes. Backbone networks typically use sampling to reduce data size, yet most existing methods rely on extracting statistical features from unsampled full traffic. In addition, asymmetric routing is widely deployed in backbone networks, where requests and responses traverse different paths, resulting in unidirectional traffic at monitoring points. None of the existing methods have considered the impact of unidirectional traffic on features.

Therefore, detecting malicious DoH in high-speed backbone networks presents the following challenges: 1) Most existing methods require grouping packets into flows and extracting features through resource-intensive operations such as packet sorting and variance calculations. Consequently, these methods are only suitable for small-scale networks and fail to promptly process massive data in backbone networks, making real-time detection difficult. 2) To address the pressure caused by massive data in high-speed backbone networks, network administrators typically employ sampling to reduce the data volume for analysis. However, the available information on malicious DoH traffic is also diminished during sampling, making it more concealed. 3) In backbone networks, requests and responses usually traverse different paths due to the widespread deployment of asymmetric routing, resulting in monitoring points collecting only unidirectional traffic, while existing detection methods are designed based on the assumption of bidirectional traffic. We propose TunnelEye,<sup>1</sup> a real-time malicious DoH traffic detection framework tailored to address these challenges for backbone networks. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

• To address the significant challenges of asymmetric routing and traffic sampling in backbone networks, we construct a unidirectional traffic feature set applicable to sampled traffic based on an in-depth analysis of DoH traffic at the protocol level. This feature set provides precise and highly explainable features for malicious DoH traffic detection.

• To process massive data in real-time within high-speed backbone networks, we design the data structure DoH-Sketch for traffic feature extraction. DoH-Sketch does not require grouping packets into flows, significantly reducing computational and storage overhead and improving feature extraction efficiency.

• We conduct extensive training and testing on both public datasets and self-collected datasets captured at the 10 Gbps campus network boundary. Experimental results demonstrate that TunnelEye can detect malicious DoH traffic rapidly and accurately, achieving a feature extraction speed of 35.17 Gbps and enabling attack detection at the second level. Compared to existing methods, Tunnel-Eye improves the average accuracy by 14.5% at a 1/128 sampling rate and reduces the false positive rate by 31% under real-world network background traffic, exhibiting stronger robustness.

## II. RELATED WORK

In this section, we review related work from three perspectives: DNS traffic analysis, encrypted DNS analysis, and malicious DoH detection.

## A. DNS Traffic Analysis

Research on DNS traffic analysis primarily encompasses DNS measurement and malicious activity detection.

DNS measurement studies investigate various aspects of DNS systems, including operational efficiency, system architecture, asset mapping, and security analysis. Pinto et al. [17] pointed out that implementation differences among DNS measurement tools can lead to biased performance results. The tool Trufflehunter [18] successfully inferred the cache architectures of public DNS services such as Google and Cloudflare through TTL timeline analysis. Li et al. [19] proposed the DNSScope framework, enabling fine-grained estimation of query arrival rates at recursive resolvers, achieving an average absolute error of 0.124. Moura et al. [20] revealed the problem of “DNS polarization” through passive TCP-based measurements. Lyu et al. [21] automatically identified and monitored DNS assets and their security health status in enterprise networks by passively analyzing DNS traffic.

Many studies aim to identify various malicious behaviors from DNS traffic. Detection for service availability mainly focuses on DNS flood and amplification DDoS threats. S¸ahin et al. [22] proposed the HELP4DNS system, which leverages a programmable data plane to implement DNS query rate limiting and firewalling on P4 switches, effectively defending against flooding and amplification attacks. Detection for data integrity focuses on tampering attacks such as DNS hijacking and cache poisoning. Afek et al. [23] proposed the POPS system to detect DNS cache poisoning attacks, reducing the attack success probability to 0.0076%. Detection of covert channels covers threats such as DNS tunneling and DGA domains. Wang et al. [1] provided a comprehensive survey of DNS tunneling detection. Manasrah et al. [24] proposed a machine learning method based on lexical features of DNS traffic to efficiently detect DGA-generated malicious domain names.

## B. Encrypted DNS Analysis

Plaintext DNS communication between clients and resolvers has long faced risks of privacy eavesdropping and man-in-themiddle (MITM) attacks. To provide transport confidentiality, the IETF standardized three encrypted DNS protocols, including DNS over TLS (DoT) published in 2016 [7], DNS over HTTPS (DoH) standardized in 2018 [8], and DNS over QUIC (DoQ) standardized in 2022 [9].

Among the standardized encrypted DNS protocols, DoH has become the most popular due to broad support from DNS resolution service providers and clients. Currently, major public DNS resolvers (e.g., Cloudflare, Google, and Quad9) support both DoH and DoT. On the client side, browsers such as Chrome [11], Firefox [10], and Edge [11] use DoH rather than DoT as the encrypted DNS solution. In addition, Apple announced in June 2020 that macOS 11 and iOS 14 natively support DoT and DoH.

Several studies have examined the reachability, performance, and security of DoH. Lu et al. [25] confirmed through large-scale measurements that DoH has achieved global reachability and that its adoption continues to grow. Borgolte et al. [26] similarly found that the number of DoH-supporting providers is steadily increasing. Regarding performance, Borgolte et al. [26] systematically measured DoH performance and pointed out that the TLS handshake is the main bottleneck. Bottger et al.¨ [27] verified that DoH has limited impact on user experience, whereas Deccio et al. [28] noted that deployment of its key optimization mechanism, TCP Fast Open (TFO), remains insufficient and may constrain performance.

With respect to DoH security, existing studies indicate that while DoH improves privacy, it also introduces new attack surfaces. Hu et al. [29] found that even after encryption, packet inter-arrival times of DoH, DoT, and DoQ may still leak information. To mitigate such traffic analysis attacks, RFC 8467 recommends padding messages before encryption; however, Bushart et al. [30] showed that padding alone is insufficient. Their method can infer websites visited by users from encrypted DNS traffic with an accuracy of 32.0%. Niakanlahiji et al. [31] further proposed a client-side obfuscation approach that combines compressive-sensing-based padding, dummy query injection, and random delays, reducing the accuracy of traffic analysis attacks from 95.58% to below 9%. Lyu et al. [32] systematically summarized the technical evolution of encrypted DNS and pointed out that malware is increasingly abusing DoH for covert communications, posing new challenges to detection techniques. Therefore, while DoH enhances privacy, it also brings new security challenges, calling for further research on attack detection and defense.

## C. Malicious DoH Detection

DoH tunneling is one of the most common forms of malicious DoH traffic. Consequently, many studies on malicious DoH traffic focus on detecting DoH tunneling activities. Existing research on malicious DoH detection can be broadly categorized into two types: rule-matching-based methods and artificial intelligence (AI)-model-based methods, with some approaches combining both techniques.

1) Rule-Matching-Based Methods: These methods detect malicious DoH traffic using predefined rules such as thresholds or allowlist. Kwan et al. [14] analyzed packet size, packet rate, and throughput to determine thresholds for distinguishing malicious DoH traffic from benign DoH traffic. Zhan et al. [33] proposed a allowlist mechanism using TLS fingerprints However, attackers can evade detection by mimicking popular TLS implementations. Additionally, Moure-Garrido et al. [34] focused on real-time detection and proposed a real-time detection system for malicious DoH traffic based on packet size and inter-packet interval, but it detected only about 3% of dns2tcp connections.

Although rule-based methods are computationally efficient, they rely on simple features and fixed thresholds, which are sensitive to traffic sampling and asymmetric routing common in high-speed backbone networks. These factors often lead to unreliable features, limiting detection accuracy and making it difficult to identify complex malicious DoH behaviors in backbone networks.

2) AI-Model-Based Methods: These methods can be categorized into deep learning-based and machine learning-based approaches.

## Deep Learning (DL)-Based Methods:

DL-based methods often rely on primary traffic features and learn complex representations through neural networks. Ding et al. [35] proposed a variational autoencoder-based anomaly detection model using sequences constructed from packet length, direction, and inter-arrival time. However, it requires reconstructing bidirectional flows, which is difficult in high-speed backbone networks. Liu et al. [36] combined Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) with CNNs to improve detection in low-data scenarios, but still relied on bidirectional flow-level features. Wu et al. [37] applied GANs to generate synthetic DoH traffic on small datasets, improving detection accuracy. However, it assumes complete bidirectional flow capture, which is difficult in backbone networks. Du et al. [38] proposed a semi-supervised autoencoder model, reducing the need for labeled data but still relying on bidirectional flow analysis. Jiao et al. [39] introduced an attention-based CNN model for IPv6 DoH traffic, which uses flow-level statistics and ignores unidirectional flow effects.

Most DL-based malicious DoH detection methods leverage deep learning’s automatic feature extraction but typically rely on flow-level features. This flow reconstruction overhead limits real-time deployment in high-speed backbone networks. Moreover, most methods assume unsampled and symmetric traffic, leading to significant performance degradation in backbone networks. DL models require large datasets and lack explainability.

Machine Learning (ML)-Based Methods: ML-based methods are more explainable and require smaller datasets for training. As a result, they are widely adopted in practical detection tasks. Zebin et al. [15] used 29 statistical features extracted from full traffic flows in the CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 [40] dataset to train a stacked Random Forest model. Some features are unavailable in unidirectional flows, while others become unreliable when traffic is sampled. Niktabe et al. [41] improved transparency using features based on mutual information and correlation, but still relies on bidirectional sequences. Wang et al. [42] combined threshold rules with ML models for malicious DoH detection, but requires complete bidirectional flows. Aggarwal et al. [16] and Zhan et al. [33] refined feature sets and classifiers, but also depend on flow-level data derived from unsampled, bidirectional traffic. Mitsuhashi et al. [43], [44] proposed a hierarchical ML framework using 28 flow-level features for DoH tunneling tool detection, evaluated only under bidirectional flow conditions. Qiu et al. [45] partially considered unidirectional traffic in experiments, but the dataset contained only nine malicious samples, leaving the method’s effectiveness in backbone networks unverified. Qiu et al. [45] partially considered unidirectional traffic, but evaluated only on a very small malicious dataset.

## III. PROBLEM BACKGROUND

Deploying malicious DoH traffic detectors in high-speed backbone networks faces numerous practical constraints. As shown in Fig. 1, monitors are placed at backbone nodes to passively observe traversing traffic. We first outline the constraints of the backbone monitoring environment and then distill the main challenges faced when deploying malicious DoH detectors in practice.

## A. Constraints on the Backbone Network Monitoring

In backbone network monitoring, two categories of constraints significantly impact the deployability of malicious DoH detectors:

1) Asymmetric Routing: Asymmetric routing is ubiquitous in backbone networks. As illustrated in Fig. 1, packets belonging to the same end-to-end connection often traverse different paths in the forward and reverse directions. Consequently, a monitor may only observe unidirectional traffic. Statistics from two 10 Gbps Internet exchange link datasets (MAWI-202006031400 and MAWI-202006101400) from the WIDE project in Japan indicate that the proportions of unidirectional flows are 84.10% and 81.55% respectively [46]. This demonstrates the widespread existence of asymmetric routing in the WIDE backbone, rendering features that rely on bidirectional interaction semantics incomputable or distorted.

2) Traffic Sampling: To constrain storage and processing overheads, monitoring edges typically implement packet sampling at fixed ratios. Sampling reduces the number of observed packets and may eliminate burstiness or short-term behavioral patterns. Therefore, feature extraction methods relying on finegrained packet sequences, inter-arrival times, or complete byte sequences may suffer from severe distortion after sampling.

## B. Deployment Challenges

Although existing malicious DoH detection methods perform well in laboratory or LAN environments, they typically assume access to unsampled, complete bidirectional flow data. However, directly migrating these methods to the backbone monitoring environment faces the following challenges:

1) Reliance on Bidirectional Flow Features: Existing methods widely utilize bidirectional statistical features. However, asymmetric routing in backbone networks results in monitors typically having only unidirectional visibility. This implies that the bidirectional features relied upon by existing methods become incomputable or distorted. Adopting these features in backbone networks leads to semantic errors, thereby causing a drastic degradation in model accuracy.

2) Feature Distortion Caused by Sampling: To cope with massive network traffic, employing sampling strategies is the norm for backbone detectors. However, existing flow features often rely on fine-grained packet sequence information. Sampling disrupts the temporal structure and sequence integrity of packets, causing these statistical features to fluctuate severely.

3) Performance Bottlenecks: Existing methods typically require maintaining per-flow states to perform flow reassembly, sorting, and statistical computation. Under the pressure of millions of concurrent connections and massive traffic volumes in backbone networks, maintaining huge flow tables consumes enormous memory, and frequent table lookups and updates lead to severe latency. In summary, detecting malicious DoH traffic in backbone networks calls for a new detection scheme. Such a scheme should offer unidirectional compatibility and robustness to sampling to cope with real-world network conditions, adopt a lightweight design to enable high-throughput online analysis.

## IV. METHODOLOGY

To achieve accurate and real-time detection of malicious DoH traffic in high-speed backbone networks, we propose TunnelEye. This framework comprises four core modules: traffic input, feature extraction, model training, and malicious DoH detection, as illustrated in Fig. 2. TunnelEye is based on the unidirectional traffic feature set we constructed, which is tailored for sampled traffic. In the following subsections, we describe the constructed feature set and each module within TunnelEye.

## A. Unidirectional Traffic Feature Set Construction

To construct features with higher explainability and precision, we analyze encrypted DoH traffic by examining the transmission pattern, working mechanism, and distinctions between benign and malicious DoH traffic.

## Transmission patterns.

In HTTPS communication, application data are encapsulated within HTTP/2 frames, encrypted at the TLS layer, and then fragmented into TLS records. Each TLS record is prefixed with a header (Fig. 3a). TLS records may be encapsulated within packets in two distinct ways according to the size of the TLS record. When the size is small and does not exceed the TCP transmission limit, such as DoH traffic, the entire TLS record can be transmitted within a single packet, as shown in Fig. 3a. Conversely, in most HTTPS scenarios such as web browsing, video streaming, and social media, the size of TLS record is often large enough to exceed the TCP transmission limit. As a result, a single TLS record may be fragmented across multiple TCP segments, leading to an entire TLS record within multiple packets, as shown in Fig. 3b. We refer to this phenomenon as cross-packet transmission. Overall, DoH traffic follows the pattern shown in Fig. 3a, whereas other HTTPS traffic corresponds to Fig. 3b.

## B. Traffic Input

TunnelEye supports two input modes for different usage scenarios in the traffic input module. During model training, it reads traffic from offline pcap files. For real-time detection, it supports analyzing traffic obtained from a traffic collector connected to the backbone network. Both input modes support traffic sampling, and the sampling rate can be configured according to network conditions.

## C. Feature Extraction

In the feature extraction module, to quickly extract the proposed features in high-speed backbone networks, we designed DoH-Sketch based on Sketch [46] as a feature extractor. Unlike traditional flow-based feature extraction methods used in prior research, our DoH-Sketch employs a streamprocessing algorithm that does not require grouping packets into flows, thus avoiding the storage of flow-state data in memory. Additionally, flow-based approaches often suffer from reduced information availability in sampling scenarios. To address this limitation, our DoH-Sketch adopts a thresholdbased cumulative feature extraction strategy.

Design of DoH-Sketch. As shown in Fig. 6, the data structure of DoH-Sketch is a two-dimensional matrix of buckets, denoted by Buckets, consisting of $d \cdot w$ buckets. Each bucket contains counters used to calculate features, allowing the simultaneous recording of multiple traffic characteristics of the data packets. The detailed information of the counters in the bucket is shown in the Table III.

## D. Model Training

In this module, we train detection models using the features extracted from pcap files by DoH-Sketch. The detection model consists of two classifiers: the malicious DoH classifier, which identifies malicious DoH traffic from network traffic, and the tunnel tool classifier, which further categorizes the detected malicious traffic based on different attack sources (malicious tunnel tools).

When selecting a model for classifiers to perform detection tasks on high-speed backbone networks, we consider both accuracy and performance. In Section V, we compared various artificial intelligence models to determine the most suitable one. During training, we applied regularization, subsampling, and early stopping to control model complexity and prevent overfitting. To minimize the impact of anomalous data, we used ten-fold cross-validation to train and test all candidate models on the publicly available CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 dataset and our DoH-Extended datasets. The model showing the best overall accuracy and speed was then chosen.

## E. Malicious DoH Detection

In the malicious DoH detection module, the malicious DoH classifier and the tunnel tool classifier, obtained from the model training module, can be deployed on the backbone network. First, DoH-Sketch extracts features from either sampled or unsampled traffic. Next, by analyzing the extracted features, the classifier detects whether a DoH tunnel attack is occurring. If a DoH attack is detected, the tunneling tool classifier further analyzes the tunneling tool used in the attack.

## V. EXPERIMENTS AND EVALUATION

We evaluated the detection performance, real-time capabilities, efficiency, and durability of TunnelEye and compared it with existing methods. This section presents the results of these experiments.

## A. Experiment Setup

Testbed: For the real-time analysis experiment, we conducted experiments on an Ubuntu 20.04 virtual machine with four processor cores, a 100GB hard drive, and 8GB RAM. In other experiments, we performed experiments on a Windows 11 system equipped with an Intel<sup>2</sup> Core<sup>3</sup> i7-12700K CPU @ 3.61 GHz, a 4TB hard drive, and 128GB RAM.

Dataset: We validated the effectiveness of our proposed method using four datasets and two mixed datasets. The four individual datasets include two public datasets and two selfcollected datasets,<sup>2</sup> as described in Table IV.

1) CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020: The publicly available CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 dataset is provided by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity (CIC). The dataset employs a two-layered approach to capture non-DoH, benign DoH, and malicious DoH traffic. Non-DoH traffic is collected by accessing the top 10k Alexa websites through a web server. Benign and malicious DoH traffic is generated using browsers supporting DoH services and DNS tunneling attack tools. The dataset includes traffic samples generated by four major DoH servers (AdGuard, Cloudflare, Google DNS, and Quad) and three DNS tunneling tools (dns2tcp, DNSCat2, and iodine) that provide DoH services.

2) DoH-Extended: To ensure that our detection method can adapt to the latest traffic patterns and effectively identify various types of DoH tunneling traffic, we constructed the selfcollected dataset DoH-Extended. This dataset was collected during multiple periods between July 2022 and November 2024.

The DoH-Extended dataset captures benign DoH traffic and non-DoH traffic using the same method as the publicly available dataset CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020. For benign DoH traffic, a portion is sourced from two DoH servers (Quad and Google DNS) in CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020, while the rest is collected from other mainstream DoH servers (AliDNS, DNSPod, 360, and OpenDNS) during our data collection. Regarding malicious DoH traffic, the public dataset CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 is limited in the coverage of attack tools, encompassing only three DNS tunneling tools, failing to represent the diversity of current DoH tunneling attacks comprehensively. To address this limitation, our self-collected dataset extends the coverage by incorporating three additional tunneling tools: DoHC2, dnstt, and DNSExfiltrator. It expands the applicability of the detection method, enabling it to identify and defend against a broader range of DoH tunneling attacks.

To collect attack traffic, we first need to construct the attack environment. Therefore, we begin by introducing the threat model, followed by the attack implementation and the traffic collection process.

Threat model. The prerequisite for establishing a DoH tunnel is that the attacker has already infected the target host through a Trojan or other means and deployed a malicious DoH tunneling tool. The attacker can send requests to a specified domain by manipulating the infected host. Since the attacker controls the domain and its resolution authority, they can design NS records to specify a controlled domain server to resolve the domain, thereby establishing a tunnel between the infected client and the controlled authoritative DNS server. The attacker can then use a Command and Control (C&C) server to transmit commands and execute attack actions through the tunnel.

Implementation of the Attack. The attacker registers a dedicated domain, “name.site,” and configures an A record pointing to the IP address of the attacker-controlled server, where an authoritative DNS service is deployed. The attacker then creates two NS records, send.name.site and receive.name.site, both delegated to the authoritative server for name.site. From the attacker’s perspective, receive.name.site is used to deliver control commands, while send.name.site is used to return (exfiltrate) sensitive data. The attack exhibits different behaviors in the waiting state and the active state.

When the DoH tunneling attack is in the waiting state, the malware deployed on the infected host periodically sends “waiting/heartbeat” status reports to the attacker. At fixed intervals, the infected host embeds the status information into subdomains of receive.name.site and issues DNS resolution requests. These requests are processed by DoH proxy/resolver providers in the network, and the embedded information is delivered to the attacker side.

When the attacker intends to steal sensitive information, control commands are embedded in DoH response messages and delivered from the attacker-controlled server to the infected host. Once the DoH tunneling attack starts, the malicious DoH tunneling client on the infected host pads and segments the victim’s sensitive data, embeds the segments into subdomains of send.name.site, and continuously issues DNS resolution requests. After being resolved via the DoH proxy/resolver providers, the exfiltrated data are delivered to the attacker. Meanwhile, the attacker can also embed new control commands in response messages and deliver them back to the infected host.

Traffic Collection. For traffic capture, we deploy Wireshark on the infected host and record traffic during DoH tunneling attacks, storing traces in pcap format. We vary multiple attack parameters to collect traces across different scenarios, including different tunneling tools (DoHC2, dnstt, and DNSExfiltrator) and different DoH resolver providers (AliDNS, DNSPod, 360, and OpenDNS). During data collection, the infected host does not run any applications unrelated to the experiment; therefore, we label malicious DoH traffic by matching the infected host’s IP address with the IP addresses of the selected DoH resolver providers, and treat the resulting flows as attack-generated malicious DoH traffic.

3) Trace A: To assess the impact of hash collisions on the DoH-Sketch in high-speed backbone networks, we used Trace A from the MAWI dataset [47]. Trace A was captured at sampling point F on the WIDE backbone network, covering 24 hours of traffic (from 23:45 on June 18 to 23:45 on June 19, 2024) on the transit link to the upstream ISP (Internet Service Provider). For privacy protection, the MAWI dataset anonymizes the data by removing payloads from TCP packets containing private user information and obfuscating source and destination addresses in the IP headers.

4) Trace B: To evaluate TunnelEye’s resistance to interference in real networks, we collected Trace B and retained the payload. Trace B consists of 500 seconds of daily traffic (totaling 112GB) captured at the boundary of a campus network with a 10Gbps bandwidth. It includes a small amount of benign DoH traffic and various HTTPS traffic but does not contain malicious DoH traffic.

Mixed Datasets.The mixed datasets were constructed by combining subsets from above datasets. To simulate a network environment where different types of traffic coexist, we mix flows in a time-aligned manner. During this process, we define two roles during the mixing process: the host flow, which serves as the temporal container, and the injected flow, which is inserted into it. Each injected flow is assigned an insertion timestamp that determines when it begins within the host flow.

Let $T _ { h }$ denote the duration of the host flow, and $T _ { j }$ the duration of the injected flow. If $T _ { h } > T _ { j }$ , the insertion timestamp is randomly chosen from the interval $( 0 , T _ { h } - T _ { j } )$ , ensuring the injected flow fits entirely within the host flow. If $T _ { h } \leq T _ { j }$ the insertion timestamp is sampled from $( 0 , \lambda T _ { h } )$ , where the mixing coefficient λ is set to 0.05 in our experiments. This allows partial overlap when the host flow is relatively short. Finally, the timestamps of the injected flow are shifted by the chosen insertion time to align it within the host flow.

1) MixedSet1: By mixing CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 and DoH-Extended, we obtain MixedSet1. In an experiment comparing performance with similar work, MixedSet1 is used for 10-fold cross-validation. In another experiment evaluating interference resistance, 60% of MixedSet1 is used as the training set, and the remaining 40% is combined with Trance B to construct the test set.

2) MixedSet2: By mixing 40% of MixedSet1 with Trance B, we obtain MixedSet2. This dataset is used as the test set in an experiment comparing interference resistance with similar work.

Evaluation metrics: F1 score, precision, accuracy (ACC), and recall are the four most commonly used evaluation metrics for classification models. In malicious traffic detection tasks, false negative rate (FNR) and false positive rate (FPR) are critical factors affecting model performance. Therefore, to more comprehensively reflect TunnelEye’s detection performance, we use F1 score, precision, accuracy, recall, FNR, and FPR as evaluation metrics.

## B. Parameter Selection

This section discusses the selection of key parameters for features and DoH-Sketch.

1) Selecting Parameters ∆L for $\scriptstyle { L _ { P L _ { - } } }$ scope N and L<sub>TLS</sub> scope N To compute the features $L _ { P L . }$ scope N and $L _ { T L S \_ s c o p e \_ N }$ , the parameter $\Delta L$ must first be determined. $\Delta L$ determines the length interval for recording the payload and TLS record length distributions, which is critical for capturing distributional differences between malicious DoH traffic and other types of traffic. An appropriately chosen $\Delta L$ can significantly enhance the accuracy and efficiency of malicious DoH traffic detection.

A small $\Delta L$ enables the model to capture fine-grained differences in payload and TLS record length distributions, which can improve detection accuracy. A large $\Delta L ,$ on the other hand, reduces the number of intervals, which decreases computational and memory overhead during feature extraction and model training. Each setting offers a clear advantage: one enhances accuracy, the other improves efficiency. However, these benefits cannot be achieved simultaneously. This section aims to choose a $\Delta L$ that balances the two.

To maximize the distinction between two types of traffic distributions while maintaining acceptable resource usage, we employ Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JS Divergence) to quantify their distributional differences and determine an appropriate $\Delta L$ . JS Divergence is a method for measuring the similarity between two probability distributions, where a JS Divergence closer to 1 indicates a more significant difference between the two distributions. Although the payload length distribution and TLS record length distribution are frequency distributions, they can approximate probability distributions when the sample size is sufficiently large. ## E. Model Sensitivity to the Threshold θ

In DoH-Sketch, the feature extraction threshold θ indicates the packet count required to trigger feature extraction. The choice of θ determines the trade-off between detection speed and accuracy. If θ is set too large, more packets must be accumulated before extraction is triggered, increasing the feature output delay and thereby slowing detection and alerting. Conversely, if θ is too small, features can be generated more quickly, but because the number of observed packets in a bucket is insufficient, statistics become more susceptible to random fluctuations and transient noise, leading to unstable features and degraded detection accuracy. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct sensitivity experiments to systematically evaluate the model’s dependence on θ, identify stable and fragile performance regions as θ varies, and provide guidance for selecting θ.

## H. Hash Collision Analysis

To analyze the effect of hash collisions on feature accuracy, we conducted the experiments. The hash function maps highdimensional data to a low-dimensional storage structure in the Sketch algorithm. Since the range of hash values is limited while the input data can be massive, different data items may be mapped to the same location, resulting in hash collisions. These collisions cause originally independent data to mix, leading to frequency estimation errors and reducing feature accuracy.

In DoH-Sketch, hash collisions occur when packets with different Keys are mapped to the same bucket. A feature conflict occurs when all d buckets corresponding to a Key experience hash collisions. Feature conflicts are closely related to the count of long flows (Long Flow Count, $L F C )$ in the network, as long flows, which contain more packets than short flows, have a more significant impact on feature conflicts. ## I. Comparison With Similar Work

We compare our work with the research by Zebin et al. [15], which proposes a Balanced Stacked Random Forest (BS-RF) model. The BS-RF model achieves accuracy, recall, and F1 scores exceeding 99.9% on the public dataset CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020, making it one of the most representative works in malicious DoH detection.

The BS-RF model [15] extracts 29 features from the CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 dataset, grouped into five categories: flow duration, flow byte count, packet length, packet time, and packet inter-arrival delay. Among these features, the packet length mode is highly contributive but flawed: the mode is 68 bytes for 88.7% of malicious DoH traffic and 66 bytes for 50.5% of benign DoH traffic. These values correspond to the typical size of TCP ACK packets, with the two-byte difference resulting from the different operating systems used during data collection. Specifically, the 68-byte ACK packets were captured on a Linux platform, while the 66-byte ACK packets were captured on a Windows platform. This discrepancy indicates that the packet length mode is influenced by environmental factors rather than intrinsic characteristics of malicious DoH traffic. Consequently, we excluded this feature from all comparison experiments.

In contrast, the BS-RF [15] model exhibits significant performance degradation due to background traffic interference, with the FPR increasing by 27.36%. The performance drop is attributed to BS-RF [15] focusing primarily on superficial features, such as packet length and packet timing, which do not capture the core characteristics of the DoH protocol. As a result, BS-RF [15] achieves a relatively high accuracy in classifying malicious DoH traffic but is easily affected by other HTTPS traffic, misclassifying non-DoH traffic as malicious DoH. By analyzing the decision paths of misclassified samples in the BS-RF [15] model, we find that the top three features most responsible for misclassifying non-DoH traffic as malicious DoH are the median packet time, the mean packet time, and the packet time skew, which indicates that HTTPS traffic with regular time intervals in daily traffic shares some similarities in packet time distribution with malicious DoH traffic. In summary, when facing the complex environment of diverse HTTPS traffic in real network scenarios, TunnelEye demonstrates better robustness than the BS-RF [15] model.

## VI. EXPLAINING MODEL DECISIONS

In this section, we employ SHAP (SHapley Additive ExPlanations) to investigate the model’s decision-making process. Based on Shapley values from game theory, it quantifies each feature’s marginal contribution to predictions, revealing the direction and magnitude of their impact. This analysis helps identify key predictive features and uncovers complex featureprediction relationships, enhancing model transparency and interpretability.

## VII. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we propose TunnelEye, a real-time and explainable malicious DoH traffic detection framework designed for high-speed backbone networks. In this framework, we construct a unidirectional traffic feature set applicable to sampled traffic, enabling detection in backbone networks with widely deployed asymmetric routing. We also design the innovative data structure, DoH-Sketch, to extract the features quickly. Experimental results show that TunnelEye significantly improves the detection accuracy in backbone network scenarios while maintaining excellent real-time performance. DoH-Sketch achieves a processing rate of 35.17 Gbps and can monitor high-speed network traffic for a long time. Even under background traffic interference, its robustness significantly outperforms similar works. In future work, we plan to extend TunnelEye to support the detection of malicious DNS over QUIC (DoQ) traffic, further broadening its applicability to emerging encrypted DNS protocols.
